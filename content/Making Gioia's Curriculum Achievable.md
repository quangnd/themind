# Making Gioia's Curriculum Achievable

Practical advice for getting through [[Gioia's 12-Month Humanities Curriculum]] without burning out. Synthesized from [[Ted Gioia's Reading Methodology]], [[Deliberate Practice]], and [[Self-Directed Education]].

---

## 1. Reframe the 250 pages as a constraint, not a goal

Gioia designed the 250-page limit *to make the course doable*, not to maximize coverage. It's what rules out *War and Peace* and forces hard editorial choices. If a week feels impossible, you're probably treating the number as a floor. It's a ceiling — finish what you can and move on.

## 2. Don't start on Week 1

[[Ted Gioia's Reading Methodology]] is explicit: read old books *first*, before you've built the tradition to evaluate them. The curriculum frontloads the hardest material (Plato, Homer, Aristotle) precisely because Gioia assumes you're already motivated. If Week 1 kills you, start at a week that catches your interest — Week 11 (Stoics), Week 26 (Shakespeare), Week 40 (Dostoevsky/Tolstoy) — and loop back. The curriculum is a map, not a locked sequence.

## 3. Slow reading beats racing

Gioia read Proust at seven pages a day. He read *Finnegans Wake* aloud. His explicit position from [[Deliberate Practice]]: the learning went deeper *because* he was slow. If you're behind on pages, you're probably going too fast to internalize anything. Drop the pace. Read aloud for dense poetry weeks (2, 35) — it changes the experience entirely.

## 4. The music and art suggestions are load-bearing

The curriculum pairs each week's reading with music and visual art. These aren't optional decoration — a genuine humanities education isn't only literary. Week 10 pairs Greek tragedy with Early Blues. Week 2 pairs Sappho with Joni Mitchell's *Blue*. The juxtapositions often illuminate the text better than commentary. Skip them and you're doing 60% of the curriculum.

## 5. Hard weeks have a designed exit ramp

The three genuinely brutal weeks:

- **Week 33** (Descartes + Spinoza + Kant) — read the *Groundwork* last; it's the most applicable
- **Week 44** (Joyce's *Ulysses*) — Gioia explicitly recommends an annotated online edition; the Week 2 Homer helps since *Ulysses* mirrors the *Odyssey*
- **Week 47** (7 authors, 52 pages) — intentionally compressed; it's a sprint, not a marathon

For those weeks: accept partial reading. The point is exposure to the range, not mastery.

## 6. Treat it as gap-filling, not completion

[[Self-Directed Education]] frames the whole project as identifying gaps and filling them deliberately. You are not expected to finish the curriculum and be done — you're expected to finish it and *notice what you want to go deeper on*. Dostoevsky gets two sentences in Week 40; Gioia says *The Brothers Karamazov* is one of his top five books of all time. The curriculum is pointing at things, not delivering them.

## 7. The hardest part isn't the reading

It's protected time. Gioia was up at 5:30am in high school. He reads before work begins, at lunch, before sleep. The question isn't "how do I read 250 pages a week" — it's "where does reading sit in my daily structure?" If it's squeezed into spare moments, it won't survive. It has to be the structure everything else fits around.

> [!tip] Where to start if you're completely new
> Week 11 (Marcus Aurelius + Epictetus) is probably the single most immediately useful week in the whole curriculum — two Stoics, one a Roman Emperor, one born a slave, same philosophy. Short, dense with applicable ideas, requires no prior context.
