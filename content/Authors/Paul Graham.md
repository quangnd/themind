# Paul Graham

## Who He Is

Paul Graham is a programmer, essayist, and co-founder of Y Combinator (2005), the startup accelerator that funded Dropbox, Airbnb, Stripe, Reddit, and hundreds of other companies. Before Y Combinator, he co-founded Viaweb in 1995 — one of the first web-based application companies — and sold it to Yahoo in 1998 for roughly $50 million, where it became Yahoo Store. This practitioner background distinguishes Graham from most essayists writing about technology and creativity: his arguments about taste, design, and what makes work good come from someone who has built things, shipped things, and hired people to build things at scale.

His essays began appearing on paulgraham.com in the late 1990s and accelerated through the 2000s. The range is unusual — startup mechanics, programming language design, the nature of taste, what distinguishes great from good work, what truths society suppresses, the structure of ambition and creativity — and the consistency of the voice is equally unusual: plainspoken, first-principles, willing to arrive at uncomfortable conclusions, uninterested in credentialism. His background as a Lisp hacker is not incidental to any of this; Lisp rewards thinking about the structure of thought itself, and that habit of mind runs through everything he writes.

## Body of Work

Graham's primary public output is the essay archive at paulgraham.com, which now spans more than two hundred essays written over three decades. *Hackers and Painters* (2004) collected the early essays in book form and introduced a wider audience to his core preoccupations: the similarity between hacking and painting as crafts, the nature of taste, what makes work great. *On Lisp* (1993) is a technical book on advanced Lisp programming — still in print and still recommended — that predates his essay career and demonstrates the depth of his programming background. He also designed Arc, an experimental Lisp dialect intended to be a maximally elegant programming language, as a kind of applied taste experiment.

Key essays include: "Taste for Makers" (2002), which argues taste is not subjective and identifies thirteen cross-domain principles of good design; "Is There Such a Thing as Good Taste?" (2021), which provides the rigorous philosophical proof via reductio and the partial-order framing; "How to Do Great Work" (2023), a lengthy synthesis of everything he has learned about producing excellent work over a lifetime; "What You Can't Say" (2004), on the moral fashions of any era and the value of noticing them; "Beating the Averages" (2001), the essay that made the case for Lisp as a competitive advantage and introduced the concept of the "Blub paradox"; and "The Age of the Essay" (2004), on how the essay form works and why the best essays discover their argument in the act of writing.

## Recurring Preoccupations

Several themes surface again and again across Graham's work. Taste and quality are central — the belief that good work has an objective character, that the difference between great and mediocre is real and can be studied. Doing great work is nearly an obsession: a large fraction of his essays circle the question of what conditions, habits, and dispositions produce genuinely excellent output. The relationship between hacking and painting — the argument that programming is a craft more similar to painting than to engineering, and that understanding craft as craft is essential to doing it well — underlies most of his thinking about software. What's true but unsayable appears in his essays on moral fashions, conformity, and the conditions under which original thought is possible. And startup mechanics, though more specific, are always in the background: how organizations form, what makes them succeed, the dynamics of ambition and risk.

## Style

Graham's prose is immediately recognizable: short sentences, plain vocabulary, no jargon, a preference for concrete examples over abstract formulations, and a willingness to start from first principles even when that means arriving somewhere counterintuitive. He tends to move from observation to principle rather than from principle to application — the essay begins with something noticed and follows the implication wherever it leads. This makes his essays feel like thinking happening in real time, even when they are clearly the product of many drafts. The conversational tone is, as he himself notes in "Taste for Makers," a sign of high craft: it takes enormous work to sound that easy.
