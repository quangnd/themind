# Taste for Makers

## The Central Claim: Taste Is Not Subjective

Paul Graham's central argument in "Taste for Makers" (2002) is a direct challenge to the polite relativism most people default to when talking about aesthetics: taste is not merely personal preference, and to say "it's all subjective" is a social convenience, not a truth. The relativist dodge feels respectful — it avoids conflict, it doesn't presume to rank people — but it collapses the moment you try to actually make something. The instant you sit down to design, write, or build, you discover that your judgment has *direction*. You make a choice, look at it, and think: no, that's worse. Or: yes, that's better. The very act of improvement presupposes a standard, however implicit. Taste can be wrong, and it can get better — which means some taste is better than other taste.

This matters because the implications are large. If taste is improvable, it becomes a skill — something to study, to practice, to learn from masters. And if great design across fields (mathematics, painting, architecture, software, prose) shares common features, those features can be articulated and taught. Graham is not arguing that beauty is simple or obvious; he's arguing that it has a grammar, and that grammar is learnable. The essay then sets out to document that grammar in thirteen principles.

## The 13 Principles of Good Design

### 1. Good Design Is Simple

Simplicity runs deeper than minimalism as an aesthetic choice. In mathematics, the shorter proof is usually the right one — not because brevity is fashionable, but because unnecessary complexity is evidence of a mistake somewhere. In prose, ornament is evasion: the writer who can't say what they mean clearly adds decoration to disguise the gap. Graham's formulation is that simplicity is not the *absence* of things but the *removal* of things that shouldn't be there. The hard part is knowing which things those are.

### 2. Good Design Is Timeless

Timeless work derives its appeal from merit rather than from the fashions of its moment. Graham's practical heuristic: if something you're making could be outdated, try to update it yourself before time does. The test is whether you're aiming at the permanent or the contemporary — and the surprising route to the permanent is often to aim at the past. What has survived centuries has already passed a selection filter; what merely trends has not.

### 3. Good Design Solves the Right Problem

Industrious misdirection is one of the most common failure modes in design. Graham's canonical example is the stove dial: arrange four dials in a row for four burners in a square, and you create a constant puzzle of which dial controls which burner. Arrange the dials in a square matching the burner layout, and the problem disappears entirely. The insight is not a better dial — it's the recognition that the right problem was spatial correspondence, not dial aesthetics. Much bad design is the product of solving the wrong problem with great effort.

### 4. Good Design Is Suggestive

The best designs leave room for the user to complete the work. Jane Austen barely describes her characters physically; readers fill in the blanks with their own imaginations and emerge with vivid, personal pictures. Lego gives you elements, not instructions for what to build. The suggestive design is one that invites participation — it's generous in a specific way, offering a starting point rather than an answer. Over-specification closes this off; it removes the user's agency and replaces collaboration with consumption.

### 5. Good Design Is Often Slightly Funny

There's a lightness to great work that signals confidence. Gödel's incompleteness theorem — which proves that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains statements it can neither prove nor disprove — has the structure of a cosmic practical joke. Hitchcock's best moments have it. Bruegel's peasant paintings have it. Humor isn't frivolity; it's the signature of someone who isn't anxious about their standing. Graham observes that the confident don't need to take themselves seriously at every moment — the work is serious, but the maker needn't be solemn.

### 6. Good Design Is Hard

Every field Graham surveys — portrait painting, mathematical proof, literary prose, engineering — rewards the work. Great portrait painters are expert face-readers; we all are, which means our threshold for noticing mistakes in a portrait is remarkably low. That demand forces precision. Hard constraints, by stripping away easy options, also strip away fashionable options — what remains has to earn its place on purely functional or structural grounds. This connects directly to [[Deliberate Practice]]: Ericsson's finding is that expertise accumulates only through effortful, uncomfortable work at the edge of current ability. Graham's "hard" principle is the design-domain version of the same truth.

### 7. Good Design Looks Easy

The paradox of mastery is that immense effort produces apparent effortlessness. The conversational tone of good prose arrives on the eighth rewrite, not the first. Leonardo's portrait heads are "just a few lines" — but those lines must land in exactly the right place, and placing them takes a lifetime of preparation. The ease is the finished product; the work that produced it is invisible. This is also why good design is often mistaken for simple design: the evidence of the effort has been removed.

### 8. Good Design Uses Symmetry

Symmetry — repetition, recursion, parallel structure — is currently unfashionable in surface aesthetics, but Graham argues it persists at the level of substructure in everything great. The Eiffel Tower is a tower made of towers. A sonnet's logic often mirrors in its octave and sestet. Recursive structure in mathematics is not decorative; it's often how the proof works. The lesson is to distinguish between surface symmetry (which can be tired and lazy) and deep symmetry (which is often the source of a design's power), and not to discard the latter in reaction to the former.

### 9. Good Design Resembles Nature

Nature has been solving problems far longer than any human designer. When organic designs appear in engineering — the tensile structures of bone, the branching logic of river deltas — it's not because designers are imitating nature superficially; it's because nature has converged on locally optimal solutions to physical constraints, and those constraints are the same constraints the designer faces. Graham's point extends to genetic algorithms: the most powerful technique humans have found for solving certain optimization problems is to run natural selection artificially. Nature isn't just an aesthetic model; it's a method.

### 10. Good Design Is Redesign

The first attempt is almost never right. The masters Graham invokes all iterated extensively, and the culture around them made iteration possible and expected. Oil paint partly displaced tempera in the Renaissance because it allowed revision — you could paint over, rework, reconsider. The same principle applies to prose (drafts), software (refactoring), and architecture (design development). The implication for practice is to cultivate what Graham calls "dissatisfaction" — not a depressive dissatisfaction, but the specific alertness that notices when something is fixable and believes it's worth fixing.

### 11. Good Design Can Copy

The greatest masters, Graham argues, become selfless in a specific way: they care more about the right answer than about credit. If part of the right answer has already been found by someone else, they use it without ego. The risk runs the other way: *unknowing* imitation — where a designer absorbs the surface of work they admire without understanding it — is almost a recipe for bad design. The copy that knows it is copying and understands why is legitimate; the copy that doesn't know it is copying is trapped. This connects to the apprenticeship model that runs through [[Deliberate Practice]] and through Ted Gioia's description of how jazz musicians learn.

### 12. Good Design Is Often Strange

Euler's formula. Bruegel's *Hunters in the Snow*. The SR-71 Blackbird. Lisp. Each of these is recognizably strange — it does something unexpected, approaches the problem from an angle that seems wrong until it seems inevitable. Graham's insight is that strangeness cannot be manufactured: you can't aim at strange and hit it. The only route is to pursue good rigorously and follow it wherever it leads — including past the point where it becomes unfamiliar. "The only way to get there is to go through good and come out the other side." Suppressing the strange impulse produces safe mediocrity; following it through good produces the genuinely new.

The strangeness principle resonates with [[Creativity as a State]] — Graham's observation that strangeness can't be cultivated, only *not suppressed*, maps almost exactly to Dan Koe's argument that creativity is a natural state buried by conditioning and anxiety, not a skill to be acquired.

### 13. Good Design Happens in Chunks

This principle is different in character from the others: it's sociological rather than formal. See the expanded discussion below.

## Where Ugliness Leads

Graham's practical starting point for the whole essay is an asymmetry that practitioners discover but theorists often miss: it is far easier to perceive ugliness than to imagine beauty. You cannot design something beautiful by imagining it into existence; you can design something beautiful by seeing something inadequate, understanding *why* it is inadequate, and doing better.

This is how most great work in history actually started. Giotto saw Byzantine icon painting — formal, static, conventional — and saw that it was not capturing what human faces and bodies actually look like in space. Copernicus saw the Ptolemaic system accreting epicycles and recognized that the accumulation of patches was evidence of a wrong foundation. Neither began with a complete vision of what they would create. They began with intolerance for a specific inadequacy.

Graham distills this into a recipe: very exacting taste combined with the ability to gratify it. The two components are separable — you can have high standards without the skill to meet them (which produces frustration and often critical intelligence), or skill without high standards (which produces competent mediocrity). The combination is what produces great work. Crucially, it is taste — the capacity to perceive ugliness, to locate inadequacy — that drives the work forward. Skill is the instrument; taste is the navigator.

## Good Design Happens in Chunks

The principle about chunks deserves more than a line. Graham's observation is one of the most practically important in the essay: Florence in 1450 produced Brunelleschi, Ghiberti, Donatello, Masaccio, Leonardo, and Michelangelo. Milan, equally large and equally wealthy, produced almost none. Why?

The answer Graham gives is environment and peer group, not genetics. A thousand Leonardos walk among us today, he writes — but without the community that makes great work normal and expected, they remain latent. The Florentine context created a specific set of conditions: patrons who cared about quality, a community of practitioners who could evaluate each other's work rigorously, a shared set of problems considered worth solving, and a cultural permission to care intensely about craft. Remove any of these, and the cluster collapses.

The historical counterparts are well-documented: the Bauhaus gathered the best designers of its generation and created a new visual language for modern life. The Manhattan Project concentrated enough physics talent in one place that problems were solved in months that might otherwise have taken decades. Xerox PARC in the 1970s produced the GUI, the laser printer, Ethernet, and object-oriented programming in roughly a decade — a density of invention that defies individual explanation.

The implication for where you work and who you work with is direct. Choosing an environment that concentrates people who care intensely about a problem is not a lifestyle preference; it is a strategic decision about whether your talent will have the conditions it needs to develop and express itself. Peer group matters not just for motivation but for calibration — you cannot exceed the standard you can see clearly, and you can only see it clearly when it's embodied in people working near you. This connects to the [[Generalists vs Specialists]] discussion of cross-pollination: the Florentine advantage was partly that painters, sculptors, architects, and engineers shared space and problems.
