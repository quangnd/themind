# Conversational Writing — Reading List

A curated list of writers and books that exemplify the "write like you talk" principle from [[Write Like You Talk]] and [[Politics and the English Language]]. These are models to study, read aloud, and absorb — not just for content, but for *how the sentences sound*.

The three traits they all share:

1. **Short sentences.** No 40-word monuments.
2. **Concrete examples before abstract principles.** Story first, lesson emerges.
3. **First person, direct address.** "I" and "you" — not "one" or "it can be observed that."

---

## Essays & Short-Form

The best place to start — short, free, high density of style per page.

- **[[Paul Graham]]** — paulgraham.com. His entire archive reads like a smart friend explaining things over coffee. Every sentence passes his own Friend Filter. Start with: "How to Do Great Work" (2023), "The Age of the Essay" (2004), "What You Can't Say" (2004). Already in wiki: [[Write Like You Talk]], [[Putting Ideas into Words]], [[Taste for Makers]], [[Good Taste]].

- **[[George Orwell]]** — "Why I Write" (1946). Even shorter than [[Politics and the English Language]]. Orwell explains his motivations in exactly the plain style he advocates. 4 pages.

- **Kevin Kelly** — *68 Bits of Unsolicited Advice*. Writes like a wise uncle at the dinner table. Short, punchy, no pretension. Each bit is one sentence of earned wisdom.

- **Joan Didion** — "The White Album", "Goodbye to All That". In [[Gioia's 12-Month Humanities Curriculum]] (Week 52). Her prose sounds like someone thinking out loud — but every word is chosen. The master of making written language feel spoken while being devastatingly precise.

- **Derek Sivers** — sive.rs. Blog posts are 200–500 words. Each one reads like a text message from a thoughtful friend. The purest conversational writing on the internet.

## Non-Fiction Books

### Writing Craft

- **Stephen King — *On Writing*** (2000). Half memoir, half writing manual. Blunt, funny, unpretentious. The single best book on writing craft that reads like a conversation. His rule: "Any word you have to hunt for in a thesaurus is the wrong word."

- **William Zinsser — *On Writing Well*** (1976). The book on clear writing is itself a model of clear writing. Reads like a patient teacher. Practices every principle it teaches.

- **Matthew Dicks — *Storyworthy*** (2018). A book about storytelling written by a storyteller — conversational, practical, full of examples. Serves both writing and speaking.

### Ideas & Thinking

- **[[Malcolm Gladwell]] — *Outliers*, *The Tipping Point*, *Blink***. Already in wiki: [[Gladwell — Outliers]]. Love him or criticize his methods, Gladwell's prose is pure [[Conversational Authority]]. He sounds like a guy telling you a story at a party who happens to have done a lot of research.

- **Bill Bryson — *A Short History of Nearly Everything*** (2003). Science writing that sounds like your curious, funny friend explaining physics. Proof that the hardest subjects can be written in the simplest language.

- **Michael Lewis — *The Big Short*, *Moneyball*, *Flash Boys***. Complex financial and technical stories told the way you'd explain them to a friend. No jargon, all narrative momentum.

- **Nassim Taleb — *The Black Swan*, *Antifragile***. Writes like he argues — aggressive, opinionated, digressive, personal. You feel like you're sitting across from him at a café. Not everyone's taste, but unmistakably conversational.

- **Derek Sivers — *Anything You Want*** (2011). 40,000 words. Reads in 2 hours. Each chapter is 1–2 pages. What he learned building a $22M company, written like he's sending you a voice note.

### Persuasion & Communication

- **Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference*** (2016). An FBI negotiator writing in plain, punchy English about persuasion. Simple vocabulary, vivid stories. Good model for business communication that doesn't sound like a textbook.

- **Tim Ferriss — *The 4-Hour Workweek*** (2007). Whatever you think of the content, Ferriss writes in extremely clear, direct, conversational English. A model for business writing without formality.

## How to Use This List

These are not just books to read — they are **models to absorb**. Three practices:

### 1. Read Aloud

Both [[Ted Gioia's Reading Methodology|Gioia]] and [[Write Like You Talk|Graham]] use reading aloud as a quality filter. When you read these writers aloud, notice how the sentences flow naturally — no awkward pauses, no tongue-twisting constructions. If a sentence sounds natural coming out of your mouth, it's a model for how to write and speak.

### 2. Copy Sentences

When you encounter a sentence that sounds exactly right, copy it into your vault. This is how you build English "chunks" at the paragraph level — not just the phrase-level chunks in your meeting files, but the structural patterns of how native speakers build arguments and tell stories.

### 3. The Translation Test

After reading a passage that resonates, try explaining the same idea in your own English — without looking at the text. Then compare. The gap between your version and the author's version is the gap to close. This is [[Putting Ideas into Words|Graham's Stranger Test]] applied to learning someone else's style.

> [!tip] Start with the shortest
> Derek Sivers's blog posts and Kevin Kelly's *68 Bits* are the lowest commitment, highest return starting points. One Sivers post per day, read aloud, takes 2 minutes. After 30 days you'll have internalized a conversational style without trying.

## The Anti-Models

To sharpen your ear, notice writing that does the *opposite*. Academic papers, corporate press releases, political speeches — anything where you can feel the writer reaching for formality instead of clarity. [[Politics and the English Language|Orwell's five diseases]] give you a diagnostic framework: dying metaphors, verbal false limbs, pretentious diction, meaningless words, ready-made phrases. Once you can spot these, you'll never unsee them.
