# The Great Divergence

[[Ted Gioia]]'s term for what he considers the most consequential binary split now forming in society — more significant than left versus right, because you cannot avoid it. The question: will you live in the real world, thinking for yourself, or will you cede your existence to algorithmic mediation?

Like every major binary opposition throughout history, it has exactly two sides. You already belong to one of them.

## The Eight Questions

Gioia offers these as a self-diagnostic — eight variants of the same underlying question:

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Do you prefer to live in the real world or the virtual world? | The foundational choice |
| Do you want to think for yourself, or let bots think for you? | Cognitive sovereignty |
| Do you want to choose, or let an algorithm choose for you? | Decisional autonomy |
| Do you *use* technology, or does technology *use* you? | Directionality of the relationship |
| Do you want the fabric of your life to be people, places, and things — or apps? | Social texture of existence |
| Do you want to be the customer or the product? | Economic relationship to tech |
| Are you the protagonist of your life, or are the burdens of agency removed from most of your hours? | *Brave New World* vs. authored existence |
| Do you want to develop skills — reading, writing, analysis, thinking — or skip all that "boring" stuff? | Investment in human capability |

All eight are the same question asked eight ways. The dividing line is not between people who use technology and people who don't — it's between people who *direct* technology toward their own ends and people who are *directed by* it.

## Why This Matters More Than Politics

Politics presents itself as the defining binary of our time, but Gioia argues it is less consequential than this one:

- In politics, your vote is diluted by millions of others; your personal impact is minimal
- You can opt out of politics entirely and it won't transform your inner life
- Technology cannot be opted out of — it seeks you out in your most private moments
- The tech divergence is a daily, hourly choice made in the fabric of ordinary life, not a quadrennial event

Even trying to find a "middle way" requires active distancing from the technocracy's ambition, which is to absorb all human vocations, activities, and eventually intimate spaces. The fence, Gioia says, will be consumed too.

## Historical Pattern: Binary Oppositions Are Inescapable

Gioia grounds this in a long view: binary conflicts have defined every society from recorded history. Herodotus and Thucydides document them. Cain and Abel. Rome's blue and green chariot-racing factions — which began as a sporting rivalry and became a civilization-spanning conflict lasting a thousand years, with citizens fighting each other while their leaders pursued authoritarianism.

The parallel is uncomfortable: we look at Rome's blue-green divide and laugh at how people chose tribal loyalty over resisting actual power. Gioia implies we are doing the same with our political polarizations — fighting each other while the real sorting happens along the real/virtual axis.

## The Divergence Is Already Visible Among Youth

The evidence that this split is underway: a 2025 survey of 20,000 young people found that **40% now voluntarily limit their smartphone use** — up from 18% in 2022. Three years. A demographic approaching a 50/50 split between people choosing to live in the world and people choosing screens.

Children growing up today face a challenge that previous generations didn't: they may need to *fight* for the privilege of a real-world life. Adults who remember pre-internet or pre-smartphone existence have a skill base for human encounters that was built before algorithmic mediation was even possible. That base is no longer automatic for anyone born after 2010.

## The Grounds for Cautious Optimism

Gioia is explicit that he sees dangers but is "guardedly optimistic." His five reasons:

1. **Each of us still possesses agency** — passivity is not compelled; it is chosen
2. **The risks of abandoning agency are becoming evident** — public resistance to tech encroachment is measurably growing
3. **A backlash is underway** — without support from CEOs or politicians (who have financial incentives to suppress it), but audible in the public
4. **Nearly half the public has already made the right choice** — they've lost faith in the technocracy and can't un-see what they've seen
5. **These people cannot be stopped from acting in their own sphere** — even if they can't change the system, they can build a human life within it

The Noah's Ark metaphor: there is room for everyone who wants to avoid the flood. The condition is simply wanting to.

## The Warning: Disabling the Opt-Out Function

Gioia's cautionary note is not alarmism but historical pattern recognition. Religious zealots and ideological movements always follow the same trajectory: proselytizing → attempting total control. When the tech authoritarianism reaches that stage, it would be described, characteristically, as *disabling the opt-out function* — a phrase that sounds anodyne but means: you no longer get to choose.

We are not there yet. Tech billionaires don't have the power to impose a theocracy. But it could change. Remaining engaged — not just opting out personally, but actively defending the conditions under which opting out remains possible — is part of what Gioia is asking for.

## Where This Sits in Gioia's Larger Project

This article is the personal-choice dimension of the same diagnosis Gioia has been building across multiple pieces:

- [[Collapse of the Knowledge System]] names the *institutional* breakdown
- [[New Romanticism]] describes the *cultural* backlash — what the successor civilization will look like
- **The Great Divergence** describes the *individual* choice — which side of that shift to stand on, and how to know which side you're already on

Together they form a complete arc: the system is collapsing (diagnosis) → a backlash is building (cultural history) → here is your personal decision point (action).

The eight questions are not rhetorical. They are a practical self-test. Anyone who answers them honestly already knows which side they're on — and whether the gap between where they are and where they want to be is large enough to require deliberate action.
