# Politics and the English Language

George Orwell's 1946 essay — the single most influential piece ever written on prose clarity. The core thesis: **bad language and bad thinking are mutually reinforcing, and the process is reversible**. If you clean up your writing, you clean up your thinking. If you clean up your thinking, you can resist political manipulation.

## The Vicious Cycle

Orwell's central mechanism:

> It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts.

This is a feedback loop, not a one-way street. Sloppy language doesn't just reflect confused thinking — it *produces* confused thinking. And the cycle compounds: each generation of bad writing makes the next generation's thinking lazier.

But the loop runs in both directions. If you can break the habit at the language level — choosing concrete words, cutting dead phrases, thinking before reaching for the ready-made formula — you improve your capacity to think.

## The Five Diseases

Orwell diagnoses five categories of linguistic decay, each with specific examples:

### 1. Dying Metaphors

Metaphors that have lost all evocative power but survive because they save writers the trouble of inventing fresh images. Examples: *ring the changes on, toe the line, ride roughshod over, stand shoulder to shoulder with, Achilles' heel, swan song, hotbed*. Many are used without knowledge of their meaning — a sure sign the writer is not interested in what they're saying. Some have been twisted out of their original meaning (*toe the line* becomes *tow the line*).

### 2. Verbal False Limbs (Operators)

Simple verbs replaced by padded phrases: *render inoperative* instead of *break*, *give rise to* instead of *cause*, *exhibit a tendency to* instead of *tend to*. The passive voice replaces the active. Noun constructions replace gerunds (*by examination of* instead of *by examining*). The result: every sentence gains extra syllables and an appearance of symmetry, at the cost of precision and energy.

### 3. Pretentious Diction

Words like *phenomenon, element, objective, categorical, effective, virtual, basic, primary, constitute, utilize, eliminate* — used to dress up simple statements and give an air of scientific impartiality. Latin and Greek roots substituted for Anglo-Saxon words (*expedite* for *hurry*, *ameliorate* for *improve*) because writers are haunted by the notion that longer words are grander.

### 4. Meaningless Words

Words used so loosely they convey no actual meaning: *romantic, values, human, natural, vitality* in art criticism; *democracy, freedom, patriotic, realistic, justice* in politics. The word *Fascism* "has now no meaning except in so far as it signifies 'something not desirable.'" These words are often used in a consciously dishonest way — the writer has a private definition but allows the reader to assume a different one.

### 5. Ready-Made Phrases

The most insidious disease. Pre-assembled word clusters (*a consideration which we should do well to bear in mind*, *a development to be expected in the near future*) that eliminate the need to choose words for their meaning. They "will construct your sentences for you — even think your thoughts for you, to a certain extent."

## The Ecclesiastes Demonstration

Orwell's most devastating move: he takes a passage from Ecclesiastes and translates it into modern bureaucratic English.

**Original (49 words, 60 syllables, all everyday words):**

> I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all.

**Modern English (38 words, 90 syllables, 18 from Latin, 1 from Greek):**

> Objective considerations of contemporary phenomena compel the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the unpredictable must invariably be taken into account.

The first contains six vivid images. The second contains not a single fresh, arresting phrase. The second is shorter in word count but longer in syllables and *says less*. The whole tendency of modern prose is away from concreteness.

## The Political Connection

This is where Orwell's essay goes beyond writing advice into something more urgent.

Political language exists to make lies sound truthful. Defenseless villages bombarded from the air becomes *pacification*. Millions of peasants robbed of their farms becomes *transfer of population*. People imprisoned for years without trial becomes *elimination of unreliable elements*.

> The great enemy of clear language is insincerity. When there is a gap between one's real and one's declared aims, one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms, like a cuttlefish spurting out ink.

The inflated style is itself a kind of euphemism — "a mass of Latin words falls upon the facts like soft snow, blurring the outline and covering up all the details."

The implication: anyone who writes clearly is performing a political act. Clear language makes dishonesty visible — even to the dishonest writer themselves.

## The Six Rules

Orwell concludes with six rules that "sound elementary, and so they are, but they demand a deep change of attitude":

1. **Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.**
2. **Never use a long word where a short one will do.**
3. **If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.**
4. **Never use the passive where you can use the active.**
5. **Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.**
6. **Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.**

Rule 6 is the escape valve — it prevents the other five from becoming a mechanical formula. The rules are aids to thinking, not substitutes for it.

## The Scrupulous Writer's Four Questions

Before the rules, Orwell offers an even more fundamental discipline. A scrupulous writer, in every sentence, asks:

1. What am I trying to say?
2. What words will express it?
3. What image or idiom will make it clearer?
4. Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?

And probably two more:

5. Could I put it more shortly?
6. Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?

## What Orwell Is Not Saying

Orwell is careful to define what he's *not* arguing for:

- Not archaism or "standard English"
- Not correct grammar for its own sake
- Not fake simplicity or forced colloquialism
- Not always preferring Saxon words to Latin ones
- Not that all abstract words are meaningless

What he *is* arguing for: **let the meaning choose the word, not the other way around.** Think wordlessly first (in pictures and sensations), then find the words that fit. Don't let ready-made phrases do your thinking for you.

## Connections

**[[Write Like You Talk]]** — Graham's Friend Filter ("Is this the way I'd say this to a friend?") is the conversational version of Orwell's discipline. Both target the same enemy: the artificial formality that creeps in when people start writing. Graham's advice is gentler and more practical for everyday writers; Orwell's is more rigorous and politically charged. Graham says write like you talk; Orwell says think before you write, then write concretely.

**[[Putting Ideas into Words]]** — Graham's "Stranger Test" (read your writing as if you know nothing beyond what's on the page) is Orwell's four questions operationalized. Both argue that the test of writing is whether it communicates to someone who doesn't share your assumptions. Orwell's version is more adversarial — the stranger is not just confused, they may be actively deceived by your word choices.

**[[Conversational Authority]]** — Orwell's diagnosis of political language ("orthodoxy seems to demand a lifeless, imitative style") describes exactly what Gioia sees dying in 2024. The shift from scripted oratory to spontaneous dialogue is the cultural-level version of Orwell's call to abandon ready-made phrases. A speaker using stock phraseology "has gone some distance toward turning himself into a machine."

**[[Clarity is Overrated]]** — The productive tension is sharpest here. Gioia argues against the *cult* of clarity; Orwell argues *for* clarity as a political act. But Orwell's Rule 6 ("Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous") and his explicit caveat that he's not concerned with "the literary use of language" leave room for exactly the ambiguity Gioia defends. Orwell fights dishonest vagueness; Gioia fights sterile precision. They share an enemy: language that has stopped serving thought.

**[[Epistemic Commons]]** — Orwell's essay is fundamentally about the epistemic commons, written 80 years before the term existed. When ready-made phrases replace thinking, the shared information environment is poisoned. When political language makes lies sound truthful, the commons is deliberately degraded. Orwell's six rules are hygiene rules for the epistemic commons.

**[[Essays as Thinking Tools]]** — Orwell's argument that ready-made phrases "will construct your sentences for you — even think your thoughts for you" is the 1946 version of Koe's warning about AI: the more you outsource thinking, the less thinking capacity you build. The mechanism is the same whether the pre-fabricated language comes from political tradition or from a language model.

> [!tip] For non-native English writers
> Orwell's rules are especially useful for someone writing in a second language. Non-native writers often over-formalize because they learned English from textbooks full of exactly the pretentious diction Orwell attacks. Rules 2, 3, and 5 are directly applicable: use short words, cut unnecessary words, prefer everyday English over jargon. Your instinct to use simpler words (because your vocabulary is smaller) is actually an *advantage* Orwell would approve of. The risk for non-native writers is the opposite of native writers: not "dressing up" language, but using dead phrases from textbooks without realizing they're dead.
