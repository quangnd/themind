# Generalists vs Specialists

> *"A jack of all trades is a master of none, but oftentimes better than a master of one."*
> (The full quote — the popular version cuts off the second half.)

The common case for specialization is that depth beats breadth: become the best at one thing and you will always be in demand. [[Dan Koe]]'s counter-argument: **specialists are attached to a skill; generalists are attached to a vision.** When skills evolve and get replaced — which they always do — the specialist loses their footing. The generalist adapts.

---

## The Structural Argument

Tools get replaced. Photoshop disrupted traditional art and design. AI is disrupting Photoshop-level skills. Each wave of technology obsoletes the prior layer of skill. The specialist who mastered the now-obsolete tool has to start over; the generalist who used the tool in service of a vision simply picks up the new one.

The apparent contradiction — "but successful people are specialists in something" — dissolves when you look more carefully. A Fortune 500 CEO, Charles Darwin, Steve Jobs: all had a specific domain they operated in. But that domain was a *vessel* for a broader vision, not the thing they were fundamentally attached to. Shakespeare is the clearest example: ostensibly a playwright, but the work required deep synthesis across human nature, language, classical literature, stagecraft, philosophy, military tactics, music, medicine, social structures. He was not a specialist. He was a synthesizer who used diverse interests as his edge.

**The distinction: specialists optimize within a skill. Generalists use skills to pursue a vision.**

---

## The Prussian Education Problem

The specialist tendency is not natural — it was engineered. The Prussian education model, adopted by America in the early 1800s to educate immigrant children during industrialization, was designed not to produce independent thinkers but obedient workers and soldiers. Its mechanisms — mandatory attendance, teacher certification, standardized testing, grade levels — were built to create *predictable, categorizable, manageable people*.

Schools optimized for specialists because that's what industrial production needed: people who could reliably perform one piece of the process without understanding the whole. Understanding the whole is the trait of the entrepreneur, not the employee — and an employee who understands the whole can replicate the system independently.

The result, generations later: most people's cognitive horizons are bounded by the specialization they were assigned, and their agency is correspondingly limited.

## Generalists as the Human Default

Humans are, by nature, generalists. This is what makes us ecologically dominant: we thrive in any niche because we can adapt with knowledge and tools, rather than being physically optimized for one environment. Put a lion in Alaska and a polar bear in the Savannah, they die. Put a human in either, and they build shelter, clothing, and hunt — because they can form a plan and execute it across unfamiliar domains.

The generalist capacity is tool-building: we are not just animals adapted to one niche. We are animals that adapt by building tools that let us thrive in any niche. This is the core of [[The 5 Human Capabilities]] — the fundamental capacities are not domain-specific; they apply everywhere.

## The Second Renaissance

The first Renaissance was made possible by the printing press. Before Gutenberg, knowledge was scarce — books were copied by hand, a single text taking months to reproduce, libraries rare, literacy rarer. Learning outside your trade required access to a monastery.

Within 50 years of the printing press, 20 million books flooded Europe. Ideas that once took generations to spread moved in months. Literacy exploded. The cost of knowledge collapsed. For the first time in history, a single person could realistically pursue multiple domains of mastery in one lifetime.

Da Vinci didn't pick one thing. He painted, sculpted, engineered, studied anatomy, designed war machines, and mapped the human body. Michelangelo was painter, sculptor, architect, and poet. These were not people who happened to have multiple skills — they were people whose diverse knowledge *created* the synthetic insight that made their primary work irreplaceable.

The internet is the second printing press. It has done to information access what Gutenberg did to book access. The cost of learning anything has collapsed to near zero. The barrier to pursuing multiple domains is now willpower, not access.

**Your edge lies more in intersection than in expertise.** A person who studied psychology and design sees user behavior differently from the pure designer. A person who learned sales and philosophy closes deals differently from the pure salesman. Every interest you pursue leaves behind a residue — a new set of connections that expands how you model reality. Specialism halts this accumulation entirely.

## Generalists and AI

The AI disruption accelerates the generalist advantage. AI can replicate specialist skills — it can code, write, design, and analyze at an expert level. What it cannot replicate is the **vision that directs the skills**.

Koe: *"If you can't create art with AI, you were never an artist to begin with. You were simply good at using a tool like Photoshop. Tools get replaced. Vision and agency do not."*

The generalist-as-vision-holder is the role that AI amplifies rather than replaces. The high-agency generalist treats AI as a new tool that reduces the cost of executing on a vision — the same way the printing press reduced the cost of distributing writing, or the calculator reduced the cost of arithmetic. In each case, the cognitive work that couldn't be offloaded became more valuable.
