# Good Taste

Paul Graham's November 2021 essay (derived from a Cambridge Union talk) provides the philosophical argument that his earlier [[Taste for Makers]] largely assumed: that good taste actually exists. Where "Taste for Makers" took the existence of taste as a starting point and mapped its properties, "Good Taste" builds the foundational proof.

---

## The Reductio

The argument is a proof by reductio ad absurdum. Start with the premise: *there is no such thing as good taste.* Follow it to its conclusions.

- If there's no good taste, there's no good art — because if good art existed, it would be easy to test for good taste by seeing who picks it.
- If there's no good art, there's no way to be good at making art — artists cannot be good or bad at their jobs.
- This extends to all art forms: no good novelists, no good actors, no good composers, no good dancers — only *popular* ones.
- And not just that we can't rank famous painters: it means **no painter is better than a randomly chosen eight-year-old**.

That last conclusion is obviously false. Therefore the premise is wrong. There is such a thing as good taste.

The proof is elegant because it doesn't require agreeing on which specific art is good — only that the category "better" is meaningful at all. Graham's own evidence came from studying painting directly: he could see that Leonardo and Bellini were vastly better than him, and that gap wasn't imaginary or socially constructed. It was the same kind of gap he recognized in other skilled work.

---

## Why People Believe Taste Is Subjective

Graham identifies two sources of the relativist intuition, both of which make sense as observations but don't prove what people think they prove.

**1. Disagreement and extraneous noise.** Most people's responses to art are not clean aesthetic judgments — they're tangled with: Is this artist famous? Is the subject attractive? Is this the kind of art I'm supposed to like? Does it hang in a prestigious museum? These extraneous factors dominate most people's reactions most of the time, generating the appearance of chaos. The noise is real; but it doesn't mean there's no signal underneath.

**2. Expert error.** The paintings that official "experts" admired in one generation are often radically different from those admired a few generations later. If the experts keep changing their minds, maybe there's nothing real there. But this proves only that taste is hard to measure and that institutions corrupt it — not that it doesn't exist. The error is conflating *difficulty of measurement* with *nonexistence of the thing being measured*.

---

## The Objective/Subjective Spectrum

The deeper philosophical puzzle is this: if art has quality, that quality must be *in* the object somehow. But it doesn't seem to be — it seems to exist only in the responses of observers. If two people disagree, who's right?

Graham's resolution: the distinction between objective and subjective is not binary but a **matter of degree**, depending on how much the subjects have in common.

The extreme objective case: a particle's mass. Everything the particle interacts with responds identically. The extreme subjective case: a pure personal preference with no shared response at all. Art sits between these poles — closer to the objective end than it feels, because **humans have a lot in common**. Our responses aren't random. And to the extent that subjects respond in the same way, that's arguably what it means for the object to *have* a property.

The vaccine analogy makes this precise. Immunity is something that happens inside individual immune systems, and immune systems vary. But it's not frivolous to say a vaccine "works" — because the variance in immune systems doesn't eliminate the meaningful central tendency. The same logic applies to art: individual variation in response doesn't eliminate the meaningful signal in the population of well-prepared, noise-free observers.

---

## The Partial Order

Graham's conclusion has an important precision to it: there is **not** a total order of artworks (every pair comparable, one definitely better). But there **is** a partial order — some comparisons are clear, even if many are indeterminate.

This matters because it defines what good taste actually is. Perfect taste would require a total order, which is impossible. But good taste — the ability to navigate the partial order correctly, to see the clear comparisons clearly — is achievable. What you develop through study and practice isn't certainty about everything; it's accuracy about the things that are genuinely decidable.

The partial order framing also explains why experts have historically gotten things wrong without that proving taste is unreal. Recent art is harder to evaluate than distant art (we lack the noise-filtering that time provides). High cultural visibility corrupts responses. Institutional consensus can be captured. All of these distort the signal — but the signal exists.

## Implications

**Taste as empirical inquiry.** The approach Graham describes — study the field seriously, compare your work to the best, notice the gap directly — is scientific in structure. You're not consulting authorities; you're making direct observations and drawing inferences. The question "is this better?" is treated as an empirical question, not a social one.

**The corruption of taste by extraneous signals.** The two sources of relativism Graham identifies — social noise and institutional unreliability — are both forms of [[Epistemic Commons]] degradation. When authority signals (prestige, fame, institutional endorsement) dominate aesthetic response, the capacity for direct aesthetic judgment atrophies. This is the taste version of the broader epistemic problem Dan Koe and Gioia describe: institutions producing consensus that diverges from reality, and individuals losing the ability to detect the divergence.

**The connection to skill.** The reductio argument's deepest implication is that taste is inseparable from skill. Graham's proof that good taste exists rests on the observation that practice improves judgment — that the painter who has studied hard is a better judge than one who hasn't. This means taste can't be separated from [[Deliberate Practice]]: you can't develop taste without developing competence, because competence is what gives you the direct perceptual access to the signal underneath the noise.
