### Or why clarity is overrated

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gfsq!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F56b876fa-4be3-4be3-8c7b-b5361d07c15d_1057x352.png)

When philosophers debate, few people pay attention.

Their eyes blur over when someone asks if the [nothing noth](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/royal-institute-of-philosophy-supplements/article/abs/does-the-nothing-noth/273A11D87334049F401D3B2FFC1159DA) s. They don't really care about the pros and cons of [immanentizing the eschaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanentize_the_eschaton). And they start nodding off when some old codger declares that [the world is the totality of facts, not things](https://philosophy.stackexchange.com/questions/8780/why-does-wittgenstein-say-that-the-world-is-made-of-facts-and-not-things).

Can you blame them?

But a recent philosophical debate on Substack stirred up a hornet's nest of buzzing and stinging. It started with the assertion that [many of the most famous philosophers are unintelligible](https://benthams.substack.com/p/how-continental-philosophers-argue).

They just don't write clearly. It's like a Christopher Nolan film—you simply can't figure out what people are saying.

I hate bad writing as much as any professional pen pusher. Even so, this controversy roused me from my dogmatic slumber. That's because some of my favorite books were dismissed as a "hideous admixture of literary analysis, gibberish, and argument."

Is this fair?

Let me present a contrary view. Buckle your seatbelts, because this will leave many of you shaken and stirred.

---

That's because I'll try to convince you that clarity is overrated.

I know this from painful firsthand experience. I fought the clarity battle on the front lines—and lived to tell the tale. Now I'll share what I learned with you.

In my early twenties, I was forced to develop a crystal clear style of writing. I didn't want to do this—it was forced upon me.

Up until that moment, I'd been a dreamer with my head full of poetry and music. And then I woke up in the most painful way imaginable.

I had to learn new words, new sentences, a whole new way of writing and talking. I eventually recovered—but it took me years to regain what I'd lost.

Let me tell you what happened.

It started when I signed on as a philosophy student at Oxford. My teachers there hated any style of philosophizing that wasn't rigorously analytic, and almost mathematical in its precision.

This meant that they ignored all the more poetic or psychological philosophers—and those were my favorites. During the course of two years, I never heard any Oxford philosopher mention the names Sartre, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Kierkegaard, or Schopenhauer.

Or Bergson or Weil or Foucault or Husserl. Or any of their disreputable buddies in France and Germany. If anyone dared ask about those forbidden thinkers, the dons responded with ridicule.

I continued to read some of these outlaws, but I learned to keep quiet about it. I dread to think what might have happened if I'd been caught with a copy of *[Anti-Oedipus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Oedipus)* in my backpack. The walls of the college might have tumbled to the ground.

I heard one eminent Oxford don refer to Hannah Arendt as a "cocktail philosopher." He wasn't talking about her alcoholic consumption—of which I know nothing—but suggesting that thinkers like Arendt (and the other outlaws named above) might be names to drop at a cocktail party. But they weren't people you could take seriously.

They just weren't rigorous enough. They didn't follow the strict rules of logic. Their writings lacked clarity.

And at Oxford, clarity was everything. If you abandoned it, you had nothing left as a thinker or writer. You were sitting naked in the middle of the college courtyard.

This disappointed me.

I greatly admired many of those un-rigorous thinkers from continental Europe. I found them inspiring and, in some instances, life-changing. [I even recited from Kierkegaard at my wedding](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/i-had-to-say-something-at-my-sons) —and was crazy enough to repeat the process when my son got married in 2024.

I recited from Kierkegaard at my wedding.

The fact that these authors wrote in a more open-ended way—serving up sentences drenched in ambiguity and subtlety—added to the fun. I was forced to do deeper thinking because of this allusive method of communication.

But it got worse after I left Oxford. That's because my next stop was the Graduate School of Business at Stanford, where the demand for total clarity was even more intense. My professors not only demanded quasi-mathematical precision in my writing, but actually preferred just plain math.

Numbers were more trustworthy than words. That was the prevalent view. And if you were forced to use words, you used as few as possible and made them crack like a whip.

The upshot is that I learned how to write with absolute clarity and total specificity.

I could turn anything into a compact three-paragraph memo. You name it— *War and Peace*, *Moby Dick*, the *Old Testament*, *The Thousand and One Arabian Night* s in the ten-volume Richard Burton translation. I could squeeze it into a single-page update—including recommendations for next steps—with room left at the bottom.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!EETq!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F73ce030b-4d03-484f-a1da-de5654d94ac3_600x400.jpeg)

The ancients consulted the Delphi Oracle for wisdom—but it came in the form of riddles.

But I suffered under these constrains. Or, rather, my writing suffered—from too much clarity. It's like a room with intensely bright lights. It might be good for surgery or a police interrogation, but it isn't a comfy place to live.

During this same period, I was making my first strides as a professional journalist and essayist. So I had to un-learn everything I'd been taught from analytic philosophy and Silicon Valley business practices.

It took me years to fix my prose. It suffered from *too much clarity.*

Don't get me wrong. I have nothing against logical thinking and clear expression. But there's also a place for poetic thinking and beautiful expression.

Writing should open up the horizon, not hide it from view.

And that's even more true now than it was back then. We are suffering from the delusion that everything in our universe can be described with total specificity. We want everything cut-and-dried nowadays, with no loose ends. We're trying to live a life without mystery or enigma.

But that isn't real life.

The power brokers want to reduce everything to computer code. That's because data is easier to propagate. Easier to scale. Easier to manipulate.

Our proper response to this should be *resistance*. We aren't data—we are human beings.

The inner stirrings of our soul cannot be framed with mathematical rigor. The depths of our emotions resist formulation in logical propositions. Many of the most important things in our lives cannot be promulgated in step-by-step argumentation.

Ambiguity has a long glorious history in human culture. It's pervasive in Taoism and Zen and other time-honored worldviews. You can find it in Shakespeare—almost in every act and scene. And in ancient Greece, people consulted the Oracle at Delphi for words of wisdom, which typically came in the form of a riddle.

By the same token, the wisest words in the wisest stories are often spoken by the Fool (see Shakespeare again) or a child (see Blake and Wordsworth). Dostoevsky grasped the same source of wisdom—and wrote it down in a novel called *The Idiot*. He understood that embracing a certain kind of nonsense (= non-sense) was the true spiritual pathway.

(Hollywood re-wrote that same story as *Forrest Gump*. But it's just one more repackaging of Shakespeare and Dostoevsky. The movie struck a raw nerve with people because it conveyed something important that is ignored by the authorities.)

In more modern times, ambiguity has been cultivated by everyone from James Joyce to Leo Strauss. And for good reason. It opens up the dialogue—instead of closing it down. It starts the conversation instead of ending it.

That's why this kind of riddling, open discourse is especially vital for education. It's the best training for students. Plato knew that long ago—it was his greatest gift to Western culture. But we have forgotten this in our own time.

We want everything crystal clear—argued out to final irrefutable conclusions. But that's not teaching. It's dogmatic ideology.

Kierkegaard laughed at anybody who would give three arguments for falling in love. Love is so important that it exists on a higher plane than argumentation. And the same is true of faith, hope, charity, and all the other glorious high points of the human condition.

The recent rise or AI-generated writing only makes this more obvious. AI can't genuinely experience these human touchstones—it can merely *pretend* to do so. So it replaces the most important facets of our lives with deceit.

The result of this is sterility and inauthenticity. Plato would call it sophistry. Sartre would describe it as *mauvaise foi* (bad faith)—lying to one's self. Or, even worse, not having a self.

When the existentialists warned about this, they were speaking poetically. But today we are literally chatting with voices that lack a self—by definition and on purpose. They are just digital constructs. Even worse, they pretend otherwise—and we are encouraged to pretend along with them.

This shift has taken place rapidly—and now the sterile AI style of writing is everywhere. Every sentence is crystal clear, although that is no guarantee of accuracy. AI is cold and confident, even when making the most foolish errors. The surface polish can't hide the shallowness and total lack of human warmth.

This is the dominant communication style of our time, whether we like it or not. Even worse, this is also the dominant *thinking* style of our time.

Most people can see immediately the danger in all this. But the personality type of the Silicon Valley CEO is strangely disconnected from human feeling and emotional intelligence. Just listen to Musk, Zuckerberg, Thiel, etc. They might be as smart as a computer, but they are also as cold as a machine, lacking the empathetic responses that are necessary for human flourishing.

They can't read the room, even if they own the whole building. That's dangerous.

This is the deadliest myth of our time—the myth of some final clarity, preferably reduced to a chatbot response. A totally closed system, with no exits or uncertainties or next steps. And we lose our humanity in this way. We end up living in a disenchanted universe.

We ought to strive for the exact opposite. At this point in history, we don't need people who think and write like machines. Instead of mathematical clarity, we want emotional warmth, psychological depth, poetic feeling, and human connection.

So let me make a plea for fuzzier writing. Let me praise the metaphors and sublime indirection of the great poetic minds. Let us seek out that open-ended ambiguity which invites us inside the text—allowing each of us to test our own interpretive skills.

Even tech advocates will soon come to crave a more human way of expression. Teachers will try to nurture it—because we often think better when grappling with a metaphor than dealing with a series of logical propositions. Employers will hire for it. Audiences will seek it out.

I'm flexible, and won't abolish that ultra-bright clarity of the chattering chatbot. There's a place for it, but a much humbler place than its advocates expect. The seat of honor at the head of the table should go to a poet or storyteller or seer.

We've always needed those inspired folks. And now we need them more than ever.
