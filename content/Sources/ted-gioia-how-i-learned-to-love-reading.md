*The Honest Broker*, January 2026. Ted Gioia traces his childhood origins as a reader — from a babysitter reading story books aloud through solo library visits, Scholastic catalogs, and a family of working-class readers — and extracts five conditions that produced his lifelong love of books. Written as a response to the documented collapse in teen leisure reading.

---

### Is this how to fix the crisis in reading?

Among the pundit class, there's been much wailing and gnashing of teeth over the decline in reading. It's especially alarming among young people—so much so that teachers are increasingly reluctant to assign entire books in high school (or even college).

The students simply won't read them. Or maybe they can't read them.

This chart has been widely shared, and it tells a sad story. Reading during leisure time among teens isn't just declining. It has collapsed.

[Chart: teen leisure reading decline — Source: Financial Times]

I'm fretting over this too.

And it's forced me to revisit my own childhood and teen years—and recall how I learned to love books and reading. Maybe there are lessons there for parents and educators today.

And I want to emphasize the *love* part. That's even more important than pedagogy and test scores. Unless a child develops a real affection for reading, all the teaching in the world will fall short.

---

When I was very young, my mother worked as a telephone operator for AT&T. This meant that I was often watched over by babysitters. I have sad memories of most of these experiences—I missed my mother when she was at work, and no caregiver could take her place.

But there was one babysitter I adored—because she read stories to me. This is the only lasting happy memory I have from my childcare experiences. But it's a delightful memory.

Even at the remove of so many years, I can still feel my sheer bliss at hearing these stories.

I think the babysitter's name was Mrs. Gardener. She would take a big stack of story books for children out of the closet—in my mind's eye I can still see it. It must have been two feet tall. And she would pick the first book at the top of the stack, and read it to me. When it was done, she went to the second—and then the third, and fourth, and so on.

I was totally in the flow state. It felt like there was an endless supply of these stories—that stack was so tall, almost as tall as me. And Mrs. Gardener kept reading them, one after the other.

No TV show had that kind of impact on me back then. No movie captivated me as much as that magical story time.

So you might say that I loved books from the start—even before I learned how to read.

But is that really so strange? All children love to hear stories. That's been true since the beginning of time.

What really needs explanation is how children lose that enjoyment—or never get a chance to experience it in the first place.

Researchers who tracked round-the-clock activities of 236,000 Americans learned that only 2% spent time reading with a child—although 21% of them had children under the age of nine.

That means that most kids don't have that blissful experience with books that I enjoyed with Mrs. Gardener. Not even at home with their parents.

But I never forgot that gateway experience, and I drew on it when I became a parent. I read to my children every night. During their childhood we went through thousands and thousands of pages.

It started with simple picture books like *Goodnight Moon*—and then we moved up to Dr. Seuss and Roald Dahl and *Charlotte's Web*. Before long, we were tackling long novels, like *Treasure Island* and *The Hobbit*. I read all the Harry Potter books—4,000 plus pages in total!—aloud to our boys—and all the Narnia books, and all the Lemony Snicket books. And many others.

Our children loved it. And so did I.

This wasn't a burden, not in the least. It was a beloved nightly ritual for all of us. This is how the love of books begins.

---

When I finally learned to read, I was delighted that I no longer needed an obliging babysitter to tell me stories. I could read them on my own. I just needed access to the books.

I was fortunate that a large city library was very close to our house. In those simpler times, I was allowed to walk there unsupervised and check out books. And I took full advantage of this privilege.

Here's another reason why I learned to love reading—I now got to choose the books myself. And the library had a huge selection. Even better, around one-third of the space was devoted to books for children and teens.

Here are some of my favorites from those years:
- Sherlock Holmes stories by Arthur Conan Doyle
- The Landmark history books published by Random House
- Nancy Drew mysteries by Carolyn Keene
- Encyclopedia Brown stories by Donald J. Sobol
- Science fiction, especially the Mars books by Edgar Rice Burroughs
- The *Dark Shadows* books by Marilyn Ross
- Books about spies, especially James Bond
- Books on chess, card magic, word games, and mathematical puzzles

I even tried writing my own stories—on 3 inch by 5 inch index cards. Each chapter filled up two cards—maybe 50-100 words. And I would write a half dozen chapters, more or less, for each "book."

So those years not only saw my blossoming as a reader, but also my birth as a writer.

---

I want to give credit to other external supports that helped me develop my lifelong love of reading.

**(1) The Scholastic Company**

Scholastic was founded when I was five years old. We never had a Scholastic Book Fair at my school, but our teachers periodically distributed a four-page book catalog from the company—and we could order books. They would deliver them at school a few weeks later.

My parents generously agreed to pay for all the books I ordered. Of course, the prices were modest—I could buy five books for just two dollars. Over the course of a school year, I might acquire around twenty books this way.

This was a big deal for me. The day the books arrived from Scholastic was magical, and I could hardly wait to start reading them.

**(2) My family**

Neither of my parents went to college. But I saw both of them reading for pleasure. So they were good role models for their children.

My father preferred history books. We owned Will and Ariel Durant's huge *Story of Civilization* in eleven volumes. He especially enjoyed the volumes on ancient Rome and the Renaissance. They fueled his pride, as a working class Italian-American.

My mother preferred poetry, and had many passages memorized. She especially liked poems that told a story. It's because of her that I can still recite lines from "The Cremation of Sam McGee" and "The Shooting of Dan McGrew."

I will emphasize that this was genuine leisure reading—pursued for fun, not for career advancement or as a class assignment.

Two other family members played a key role. My Uncle Ted died in a plane crash before I was born, but I benefited from the piano and books he left my parents. He was a genuine working-class intellectual, and his surviving library, in various languages, gave me a sense of what serious reading was all about.

And my older brother Dana did the same. He was always lending me books to read, or telling me which authors to check out. He had a bigger role on my developing literary tastes than anybody.

**(3) A used bookstore on Hawthorne Boulevard**

A hundred yards away from the public library, a small used bookstore set up shop on Hawthorne Boulevard around the time I turned eleven or twelve.

I would spend an hour browsing, and then spend seventy cents. That would get me two used paperbacks in poor condition.

The owner often tried to tempt me into spending more. Several times he told me: "If you give me ten dollars, I will let you take home a hundred books."

I got to pick the books—nobody was trying to "improve" me with a planned reading program. In fact, many of the books I got from the store were trashy genre books. Some of them were probably too explicit for a youngster my age. But the owner never censored my choices, and this probably added to my sense that books provided some liberation from rules and conventions.

---

This takes me up to age thirteen—and that was the moment I started reading for my education and mind expansion.

During the summer before I started high school, somebody gave me a list of books that would prepare students for high school. It had around 50 or 60 titles on it—and I took this list very seriously.

There were books by Ernest Hemingway, James Baldwin, Harper Lee, John Steinbeck, and many other esteemed authors. During that summer, I tried to read as many of these titles as possible. This marked an important transition in my life.

I no longer picked fun books at random. I started reading with discipline and purpose. I now decided that even difficult books were worth tackling, because they would help me develop my thinking skills.

So around this time, I also started reading Homer, Plato, Shakespeare, and other authors that challenged me, and took me outside my comfort zone. I quickly learned that this was exciting and mind-expanding.

That was the beginning of my adult life as a reader—and it continues uninterrupted until the current day.

This was only possible because:

1. I was exposed to the joy of storytelling at a very early age.
2. I soon learned how to read for fun, and discovered that reading was enjoyable.
3. I had people in my life who were role models, and embodied reading as a leisure time pursuit.
4. I had easy access to books, even in my working class neighborhood.
5. I gradually gained confidence that even tough books were worth reading.

Can those five things still work today? Of course they can—especially if we put away the digital devices for a few hours each day.

Children love stories. It's hardwired into their hearts and souls. We just need to nurture that inborn drive, and give it an opportunity to develop.

And if we don't do that for the next generation, the fault lies with us.
