The final installment. Weeks 40–52: from Dostoevsky and Nietzsche through Joyce, Kafka, Freud, and up to contemporary fiction. The last weeks are especially wild — more controversial, less consensus, more directly connected to how we live now.

---

## WEEK 40
**Dostoevsky:** "The Grand Inquisitor" (from *The Brothers Karamazov*)
**Nietzsche:** *Beyond Good and Evil*
**Tolstoy:** *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*

Three of the most probing minds of the 19th century on tough choices and moral dilemmas. "The Grand Inquisitor" — a self-contained section from Dostoevsky's greatest novel — reads like a Platonic dialogue updated for contemporary life. *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* is the most riveting book about end-of-life issues you are likely to encounter, forcing readers to confront their real priorities and values. Nietzsche wants to topple the entire moral code of Western society.

Music: Shostakovich: *Symphony 5*; Prokofiev: *Piano Concerto 3*; Rachmaninoff: *Piano Concerto 3*
Art: Bauhaus

---

## WEEK 41
**Henry James:** *The Spoils of Poynton*
**Proust:** "Overture" from *Swann's Way* (~50 pages, C.K. Scott Moncrieff translation)

Fiction doesn't get deeper or more profound than this. The Proust extract includes the most famous scene in his entire oeuvre — the madeleine. The Henry James novel is an entry point into his glorious late prose style without the page-count demands of the three great novels (*The Ambassadors, The Wings of the Dove, The Golden Bowl*). It also anticipates relationships in our disconnected day. Don't be discouraged if you find these challenging — everybody does.

Music: Mahler: *Symphonies 2, 5, 9*
Art: Henri Matisse

---

## WEEK 42
**Baudelaire:** Selected Poems
**Flaubert:** "A Simple Heart"

The darker side of Romanticism — *realism* and *naturalism* — emerged around the midpoint of the 19th century. A melancholy and sometimes dysfunctional vision of real life, but producing some of the most penetrating literary works. Baudelaire's poetry and Flaubert's short tale dig into the subterranean currents of the human psyche.

Music: Origins of country music (Jimmie Rodgers, Carter Family, Patsy Cline, Johnny Cash, Hank Williams, Loretta Lynn, Willie Nelson)
Art: Rembrandt

---

## WEEK 43
**Frederick Douglass:** *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*
**W.E.B. Du Bois:** *The Souls of Black Folk*

Foundational works about the African-American experience, and inspiring stories of human endurance. Douglass escaped slavery in 1838 and grew into a leading social activist and public intellectual — at a time when learning to read was forbidden to most African-Americans. Du Bois updates this with an expansive treatment of life post-Emancipation (1903) — historical, sociological, and aspirational.

Music: Louis Armstrong; Duke Ellington; Billie Holiday
Art: The Harlem Renaissance

---

## WEEK 44
**James Joyce:** "The Dead" (from *Dubliners*)
**James Joyce:** *Ulysses*, Episode 1 ("Telemachus")
**Samuel Beckett:** *Waiting for Godot*

Joyce strove more intensely than anyone to push writing to its limits. "The Dead" uses conventional narrative but with extraordinary expressive force — one of the finest short stories in English. "Telemachus" defies all expectations of how tales should unfold. The Week 2 reading of Homer's *Odyssey* helps — it's the model — but only so far. Beckett is easier to digest but *Waiting for Godot* is so open-ended in its implications that it also presents challenges.

## WEEK 45
**Virginia Woolf:** *To the Lighthouse*
**T.S. Eliot:** "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock," *The Waste Land*

Stream-of-consciousness continues. Woolf's sentences are poetic and psychologically charged — more than just storytelling — but once you get into the flow you move faster than you expect. Gioia's advice: pay more attention to tone, ambience, and feeling, even when the words are complex. The same holds for Eliot's poetry — the emotional currents are often direct even when explicit meanings are obscure.

Music: George Gershwin: *Rhapsody in Blue, An American in Paris*; Ella Fitzgerald: *The George and Ira Gershwin Songbook*
Art: Edward Hopper; Frida Kahlo

---

## WEEK 46
**Freud:** *An Outline of Psychoanalysis*; *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*

Freud had more impact on conceptions of human psychology than any thinker in history. Despite decades of backlash (not entirely unjustified), we still operate under the premise that inner life can be ruled by a tyrannical unconscious that may be mapped, diagnosed, and treated. This week also explores his most controversial concept: *thanatos*, the death instinct — which helps explain seemingly paradoxical elements of our self-destructive times.

Music: Messiaen: *Quartet for the End of Time*; Schoenberg: *Transfigured Night*; Barber: *Adagio for Strings*
Art: Surrealist painters

---

## WEEK 47
**Ellison:** *Invisible Man* (Prologue and Chapter 1)
**Faulkner:** *The Sound and the Fury* (Section 1, "April Seventh 1928")
**Fitzgerald:** "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz"
**Hemingway:** "The Killers"
**Shirley Jackson:** "The Lottery"
**Flannery O'Connor:** "A Good Man Is Hard to Find"
**O. Henry:** "The Gift of the Magi"

Rapidfire tour of American fiction — seven authors, five short stories, two novel extracts. The Faulkner is the longest and most challenging, but the program is not dumbed-down. Each of these authors helped liberate US culture and set a model for rule-breaking writing worldwide.

Music: Origins of modern jazz (Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk)
Art: Abstract Expressionism

---

## WEEK 48
**Borges:** *Ficciones*
**Kafka:** "Metamorphosis"
**García Márquez:** *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (Chapter 1)

Magical realism — a literary genre that has spilled into film, television, and video games. Kafka and Borges laid the groundwork; García Márquez gave it full legitimacy and paved the way for similar minglings of fantasy and realism worldwide (Rushdie, Murakami, etc.).

## WEEK 49
**Simone de Beauvoir:** *The Second Sex* (Introduction + Chapter 1 "The Data of Biology")
**Michel Foucault:** "The Eye of Power" from *Power/Knowledge*
**René Girard:** "Why Do We Fight? How Do We Stop?" from *Conversations with René Girard*

Three short but rich extracts: feminism, post-modernism, and mimetic desire. These provocateurs serve as invaluable guides to contemporary life — from Facebook to dating to the surveillance state.

Music: Woody Guthrie; Bob Dylan; Joan Baez
Art: Andy Warhol; Roy Lichtenstein

---

## WEEK 50
**Susan Sontag:** "Notes on Camp," "Against Interpretation" (from *Against Interpretation*); "In Plato's Cave" (from *On Photography*)
**Walter Benjamin:** "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (from *Illuminations*)
**José Ortega y Gasset:** *The Revolt of the Masses* (Chapters 6 & 8); "The Dehumanization of Art"

Theory week — three of the smartest, most penetrating thinkers of the 20th century. Challenging, but these readings reveal the hidden meanings in many current-day issues and conflicts.

Music: The Motown Sound and Hip-Hop
Art: Photography (Ansel Adams, Diane Arbus, Henri Cartier-Bresson, Walker Evans, Edward Weston)

---

## WEEK 51
**Aldous Huxley:** *Brave New World*

Science fiction has become necessary to understand our tech-defined lives. Huxley has proven the most accurate predictor of them all — warning of a society controlled by drugs, genetic manipulation, social conditioning, passivity, and imposed conformity. His central lesson: technological progress can coexist with social stagnation — and, in fact, may cause it.

Music: Electronic music
Art: Jean-Michel Basquiat

---

## WEEK 52
**Octavia Butler:** "Bloodchild" (from *Bloodchild and Other Stories*)
**Joan Didion:** "The White Album" (from *The White Album*)
**David Foster Wallace:** Section 22 from *The Pale King* (sometimes published separately as "The Awakening of My Interest in Advanced Tax")
**Alcoholics Anonymous:** Chapter Five "How It Works" (from *The Big Book*)
**Tim O'Brien:** "The Things They Carried" (from *The Things They Carried*)

The finale returns to the etymology of *humanities* — derived from *human beings* and *humanistic thinking*. Each of these stories tells of a person at the brink, struggling to find a way back to wholeness. Gioia gives away the DFW short novel to young people regularly. Didion suffered similarly to Wallace but found a pathway to recovery. Butler and O'Brien offer gritty stories of individuals dealing with intense situations — one phantasmagorical, one all too realistic. The AA chapter is "the most influential guide to recovery of the last hundred years."

Music: Stephen Sondheim
Art: The paintings of Leonardo da Vinci
