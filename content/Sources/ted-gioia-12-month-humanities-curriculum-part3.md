Ground rules unchanged: ~250 pages/week, music suggestion, visual arts suggestion, roughly (not strictly) chronological.

## WEEK 27
**Shakespeare:** *A Midsummer Night's Dream, Romeo and Juliet, The Tempest*

More Shakespeare — romantic fare this time. Gioia makes no apology for the attention. In an age with no psychologists or textbooks on human development, Shakespeare probed the subconscious with profundity that still stands out. His characters are simultaneously real and mythic, in the moment and eternal archetypes. He did this while entertaining a mass market with less formal education than today's average consumer of culture.

## WEEK 28
**Giorgio Vasari:** *Lives* of Giotto, Botticelli, Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael, and Michelangelo
**Benvenuto Cellini:** *Autobiography* Chapters 1–18

The Renaissance changed notions of arts and culture, but also of what a holistic, well-rounded life looks like. You can't understand the shift by looking at paintings alone — you need to probe the lives of the artists. Vasari is our original source for most of what we know. Cellini more than anyone lived up to the image of "Renaissance man" — his life would suit a Hollywood adventure film.

## WEEK 29
**Shakespeare:** *Henry IV Parts 1 & 2, Othello*

Final Shakespeare — history plays and one last tragedy. Valor, frailty, envy, vengeance, debauchery, comedy, and much else at its highest pitch. Humanists, political leaders, business executives, teachers, and lovers all need to read this.

Music: Lute music by John Dowland and Thomas Campion
Art: Pre-Columbian architecture

## WEEK 30
**Cervantes:** *Don Quixote* Chapters 1–5
**Molière:** *Tartuffe* (Richard Wilbur translation recommended)

Cervantes destroyed the brand franchises of his day — stories of chivalrous knights, the Marvel superheroes of the era — by showing what happens when an ordinary person takes them too seriously. Is Don Quixote comic or tragic? Perhaps we admire him all the more for pursuing dreams the rest of us have abandoned. Cervantes sets the groundwork for the evolution of the novel through satire, realism, and postmodern deconstruction.

Music: Piano music of Isaac Albéniz and Enrique Granados
Art: El Greco, Diego Velázquez, Francisco Goya

## WEEK 31
**Machiavelli:** *The Prince*
**Rousseau:** *The Social Contract*

Two short treatises on politics — vastly influential, entirely divergent. One pragmatic, one idealistic. Our current political situation can be traced back to both antecedents. Read them together and consider what a debate between Machiavelli and Rousseau would look like.

Music: Mozart: Piano Concertos 20 & 23; Serenade 13 (*Eine Kleine Nachtmusik*)
Art: Auguste Rodin

---

## WEEK 32
**Rousseau:** *Confessions* Book 1 (~40 pages)
**Voltaire:** *Candide*

Rousseau's *Confessions* caused a sensation — the most honest memoir he claimed to write, and he delivers. Most influential autobiographical work since Augustine's *Confessions*; every later "raw" personal narrative owes it something. Voltaire's *Candide* — short, satirical, philosophically loaded — represents a milestone in the secularization of Western culture. Both authors died in 1778, a few days apart.

Music: French impressionist composers (Debussy, Ravel)
Art: French impressionist painters

---

## WEEK 33
**Descartes:** *Discourse on the Method*
**Spinoza:** *Ethics* Part 1 ("On God")
**Kant:** *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*

Three dense, brief texts — three of the most important concepts in the history of thinking:
- **Descartes's cogito** — "I think, therefore I am" — groundwork for the Age of Reason
- **Spinoza's pantheism** — God encompassed by nature; role model for secular and non-denominational worldviews
- **Kant's categorical imperative** — Gioia's pick for the most important work of moral philosophy from the last thousand years; the superior alternative to utilitarian frameworks like Effective Altruism

Go slowly. This is what mind-expanding reading is all about.

Music: Mozart opera overtures and selected arias
Art: Japanese art and architecture

## WEEK 34
**Goethe:** *The Sorrows of Young Werther*

A scandal and a turning point — the signal that the Enlightenment had reached cultural exhaustion. Many readers committed suicide in emulation of the title character. Goethe legitimized human feelings in the face of rationalism gone wild, setting wheels in motion that eventually led to the end of slavery, backlash against colonialism, improved working conditions, and the end of child labor.

Gioia draws a contemporary parallel: rising suicides and self-harm among youth may be eerily recreating a similar response to the overly rationalized mandates of the digital economy.

Music: Brahms: Symphonies 1–4
Art: Sturm und Drang painting

## WEEK 35
**Romanticist Poetry:** Keats, Wordsworth, Blake, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, Novalis, Hölderlin

Around 1800, the leading thinkers of Europe rejected excessive scientism and brutal industrialization, turning instead to art, poetry, nature, and music — Romanticism. Poets and composers became celebrities. England and Germany were the epicenters. The backlash against human exploitation that followed is one of the most consequential cultural shifts in Western history.

Music: Beethoven: *Symphony 6 (Pastoral)*; Chopin: Nocturnes, Preludes
Art: Caspar David Friedrich; John Constable

## WEEK 36
***The Declaration of Independence* and *U.S. Constitution***
**Marx & Engels:** *The Communist Manifesto*
**Mary Wollstonecraft:** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*

The Enlightenment put all traditional hierarchies in question, unleashing competing social and political agendas. Three of the most influential foundational documents of democracy, communism, and feminism — all to be read for thinking, not compliance. Refuting is part of the intended program.

Music: Bluegrass (Bill Monroe, Flatt & Scruggs, Alison Krauss, Ralph Stanley)
Art: Mary Cassatt, Georgia O'Keeffe, Thomas Eakins, Winslow Homer

## WEEK 37
**Jane Austen:** *Pride and Prejudice*

The most imitated and ripped-off book on the entire list. Austen defined the terms of the modern love story. Seemingly traditional and embedded in her time, yet endlessly translatable. Hollywood has been adapting Austen-inspired stories for over a century — the 2005 film version is one of the few successful literary-to-film translations.

Music: Ella Fitzgerald: *The Cole Porter Songbook, The Irving Berlin Songbook, The Jerome Kern Songbook*
Art: Textile art

---

## WEEK 38
**Darwin:** *On the Origin of Species* Chapters 1–4
**John Stuart Mill:** *On Liberty*

The evolutionary mindset and the libertarian concepts that still shape current thinking. The rational currents of the Enlightenment confronted by the humanistic reawakening of the Romanticists produced a dizzying array of proposals — Darwin and Mill among the most enduring.

Music: The Waltz (Johan Strauss II, Chopin, Bill Evans, John Coltrane)
Art: Art Nouveau and Art Deco

## WEEK 39
**Emily Dickinson:** Selected Poems
**Poe:** "The Raven," "The Fall of the House of Usher"
**Melville:** "Bartleby the Scrivener"; *Moby Dick* Chapter 1
**Thoreau:** *Walden*
**Twain:** *Huckleberry Finn* Chapters 1–6
**Whitman:** "Song of Myself"

![[Attachments/gioia-emily-dickinson.jpeg]]

American literature struggled to break free from European models — but by the 19th century, New World voices became independent, cranky, and unruly. Six authors who redefined American literature. Each liberated US culture and set a model for quirky, rule-breaking writing worldwide. Poe was more influential in Europe than in the US.

Music: Miles Davis and John Coltrane, selected tracks
Art: Grandma Moses and American folk art
