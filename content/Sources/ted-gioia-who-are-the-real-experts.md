*The Honest Broker*, April 2025. Q&A format. Gioia argues that expertise and credentials are frequently decoupled — and that the direction of causation matters enormously. His central aphorism: "==Expertise is the credential. The credential is not the expertise.==" He supports this with his own biography, historical examples of uncredentialed masters, consulting experience, and five practical tests for judging indie experts.

---

### Q&A on the people I trust—and why

The scarcest thing in the world right now is trust. Institutions and organizations live and die by this single metric.

---

**You write a lot about outsiders and people on the fringes of culture—and you often criticize established institutions. Do you distrust experts?**

TED: No, the exact opposite is true. I respect expertise. But I think that some outsiders have more expertise than insiders.

**What makes you think that?**

TED: Maybe it started with my childhood and teen years. I grew up in a working class neighborhood, and I met people with tremendous expertise—but they had few credentials and no impressive institutional affiliations.

> **"Expertise is the credential. The credential is not the expertise."**

So I never make distinctions on the basis of titles and degrees. Sometimes they correlate with expertise, but many times they don't.

**Who were those intellectual influences in your home town?**

TED: Some were members of my family. I've talked elsewhere about my older brother Dana—who was a phenom even as a teenager. He taught me more about arts and culture than any of my professors. I also had two uncles—one on the Italian side of my family, and the other on the Mexican side—who were genuine working class intellectuals. They were as formidable as any professor you will meet.

These people really do exist outside of academia—even in working class towns.

**But trained academics must have more expertise than these homegrown thinkers?**

TED: I'm not so sure about that. In my own field, music, I've met freelancers and fans who know as much, or even more, than scholars with doctorates.

I fall into that category myself. I don't have a degree in music, and never took a single lesson or class in jazz—and that's the area in which I'm best known.

**How did you get hired by Stanford's Department of Music if you don't have a music degree?**

TED: I launched my teaching career during my junior year as an undergraduate—for free. I taught jazz without a salary for several years. It's amazing what you can get away with if you're willing to work without pay. There's a lesson there.

There's another lesson here—namely that you will get rewarded if you can figure out how to operate outside of the bureaucracy.

My classes were very successful. Around the time I turned 25, I actually got paid for it. Then I finished the draft of my first book, published by Oxford University Press. I made my first recording as a jazz pianist. Stanford's Department of Music got a sizable donation to run a real jazz program. I was an obvious candidate because I was the only person with a track record of teaching the subject to Stanford students.

So I left the Boston Consulting Group, taking a huge paycut, and joined the Stanford faculty the same day as Stan Getz.

My track record and my expertise were my credentials.

**But surely your case is a rare exception?**

TED: Many of the intellectuals who shaped my own thinking were outsiders without PhDs. Consider Susan Sontag, who never got her doctorate, but was the most celebrated literary critic of her generation. The same is true of Northrop Frye and Edmund Wilson, both critics of immense stature.

C.S. Lewis taught at Oxford for 29 years, and was famous all over the world. But his title was just *tutor*. He needed to leave Oxford for Cambridge in order to become a professor — at age 55.

George Steiner — one of the most illustrious polymaths of my lifetime — had his doctoral thesis initially rejected at Oxford. He turned it into a famous book, *The Death of Tragedy*, but never really got accepted by insiders. Even after a half-century of publishing and lecturing at the highest level, he faced intense hostility from professional academics. His official title at Cambridge was *Extraordinary Fellow of Churchill College*.

At least that was an accurate title. He really was extraordinary.

Anthony Quinton, philosopher, president of my Oxford college — he admitted to me he had no doctorate, though this hadn't stopped him from becoming head of the Aristotelian Society, Royal Institute of Philosophy, and chairman of the British Library.

Sir Isaiah Berlin went straight from studying PPE to teaching it — no doctorate — and nobody ever doubted his expertise.

**But you're surely cherry picking here…**

TED: Even those with PhDs often face a backlash from the bureaucracy. Lionel Trilling had a very hard time getting tenure at Columbia — for my generation, he was a legend who did more to elevate humanities there than anybody. The best faculty members often have the most enemies inside the bureaucracy.

University politics are far more poisonous than anything I ever saw in the corporate world.

**What about scientists? Don't they need credentials?**

TED: I suspect many scientists are forced into institutional bureaucracies to access facilities and funding. But those bureaucracies create groupthink and obstacles to scientific advances. That may be why the technological revolutions of our lifetime have come from entrepreneurial startups, not large universities or government agencies.

If you made a list of the Stanford and Harvard students who have had the biggest impact on technology, at least half of them would be dropouts.

**Okay — how do we determine who has expertise if we don't trust formal credentials?**

TED: This isn't as big a challenge as you think. It's often fairly obvious who the real experts are.

I learned this doing consulting at BCG and McKinsey. On my first big project, the smartest person in the whole company was a factory manager thousands of miles from HQ. On another project, it was a star sales rep out on the road. They were more informed than the CEO — and had better judgment, too.

And here's another amazing fact: all the bosses and coworkers knew these people were experts. Everyone was constantly asking them for advice.

Positional power is not the same as true expertise. And expertise always earns respect, even if it doesn't come with a fancy job title. People with the highest level of expertise are very rare. So they stand out, even if they lack an impressive degree or prestigious institutional affiliation.

**But institutions are under attack right now. So are things getting better?**

TED: The goal should be to fix them, not punish them. There's too much punishing in our society right now—and too little fixing.

But indie culture is where all the energy and momentum is right now. And I don't see that changing anytime soon.

**Why is that shift happening? What's so good about being an outsider?**

TED: This gets back to the issue of trust.

Readers can see that I don't take advertising money. I don't take endorsement money. I don't have institutional overseers or gatekeepers controlling what I say. So I can talk straight—directly to them. And *for* them.

That's a huge advantage in a world where trust is scarce.

But this only works if the outsiders really are experts. We're fortunate that some of them really are.

**How can we tell that?**

TED: Let me suggest five ways of judging the reliability of these indie experts:

1. Pay attention to which indie voices correctly predict the future.
2. See which ones identify key issues before others notice them.
3. See which ones tell you truths that insiders won't mention.
4. See which people offer coherent explanations of situations that others find confusing.
5. And, finally, see who is willing to speak out bravely in the face of powerful embedded interests.

When you find people who do that, you are in safe hands. They are the real experts.

**Do you have any final comments?**

TED: What I'm suggesting is simply a return to what worked in the past. Mainstream culture has always benefited by learning from the counterculture—that's always been true in periods of vitality. We just need to learn how to do that again.

If we do it right, everybody wins.
