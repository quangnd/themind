# Agency

> *"The tendency to initiate action towards a goal without outside prompting, instruction, or permission."* — Devon Eriksen

> *"To have agency is to be the subject of a sentence, rather than its direct object."* — Devon Eriksen

Agency is the #1 skill in [[The Post-Labor Skill Stack]] and, per [[Dan Koe]], the only skill guaranteed to remain relevant regardless of what AI does to every other domain. If you can set your own direction, do what's required to achieve it, and adapt when the environment changes, you cannot be permanently displaced — not by AI, not by shifting job markets, not by anything external.

The critical distinction: agency is not just *action*. Anyone can start a business or begin a project. What separates high-agency people is **iteration** — the commitment to learning from failure and adjusting without being seduced back into comfortable conformity because "it's not working."

---

## What Agency Is Not: Conformity

To understand agency, start with its opposite. Conformity is when your mind is still connected by an umbilical cord to society — judging truth based on popularity and acceptance by others rather than through your own direct experience or independent investigation.

The [[Ego Development Stages]] framework documents that ~50% of the population operates at the **conformist stage** (Stage 3) — meaning half the population literally lacks the cognitive architecture for genuine agency. They can conform to the idea that they "should have more agency," but that is itself a Stage 3 behavior.

Conformity stems from survival. Humans don't only survive physically (reproducing genes) but psychologically (reproducing beliefs, ideas, and identity). A job provides survival; threaten the job and you trigger fight-or-flight around conforming to whatever protects it. Hard-set ideological beliefs provide psychological safety; challenge them and the same response fires.

This means even people who identify as "high agency" can simply be conforming to what's fashionable in their circle (tech culture, entrepreneurship culture, self-improvement culture). True agency is evident in behavior that diverges from what would have been predicted.

## The Three Traits of High-Agency People

### 1. They Iterate Without Permission

Low-agency people wait for someone to assign them a path, validate their direction, or guarantee an outcome before committing. High-agency people act, fail, adjust, and act again — without needing the loop to start with external permission and without quitting when early results are poor.

The commitment to iteration is what distinguishes genuine agency from simple initiative. Starting is easy; most people start. The high-agency trait is the willingness to stay in the experimental cycle past the point where most quit — through failure, embarrassment, and the long periods of nothing working.

### 2. They Treat Life as One Giant Experiment

Low-agency thinking (the "employee mindset") assigns credit and blame to external sources: the method promised results and didn't deliver, so the method is wrong, or the goal is impossible, or they were unlucky. High-agency thinking treats everything as a hypothesis to be tested and falsified.

The experimental mindset reframes failure completely: failure is not evidence that the goal is impossible, it is data that eliminates one approach and points toward another. The question is never "did this work?" but "what does this tell me about what to try next?"

This requires expecting to fail. Not as a performance of humility but as a structural feature of the process. If you don't expect failure, failure feels like evidence you should stop. If failure is part of the expected sequence, it feels like progress — the elimination of one more dead end.

### 3. They Believe in the Difficult

Goals exist on a spectrum:
- **Easy goals** — achievable with current skills and resources
- **Difficult goals** — not achievable now, but achievable if you acquire the right skills and resources
- **Impossible goals** — outside the realm of current possibility, or not accessible until a chain of difficult goals is completed first

The trap: [[Learned Helplessness]]. People who have been repeatedly exposed to failure in a specific domain — especially early, especially from authority figures — stop perceiving difficult goals as achievable. They bear the discomfort of their current situation rather than even attempting to escape, because they have been conditioned to believe escape isn't possible.

High-agency people have either avoided this conditioning or broken through it. They see difficult goals as temporarily blocked, not permanently impossible. Any goal can be achieved on a long enough timescale if you have the [[Cybernetics|cybernetic capacity]] to iterate toward it.

## Agency as an Art Form

> *"In ordinary practical life, we usually take the means for the sake of the ends. But in games, we can take up an end for the sake of the means."* — C. Thi Nguyen

Painting records sights. Music records sounds. Stories record narratives. Games record *agencies* — they are the art form that captures and transmits how to pursue a goal in a constrained environment.

This is why [[The Video Game Framework]] works: games are the most studied model of voluntary, sustained, intrinsically motivated effort. When you play someone else's game, you're learning their agency in a structured context. The progression from "playing by the rules" to "creating your own rules" is the trajectory of agency development.

---

## How to Practice Agency

You develop agency by studying others' agencies until you are capable of creating your own. The practical process:

1. **Research processes** that others have found success with — YouTube, social media, courses, mentors. You're collecting tested hypotheses.
2. **Experiment** with various techniques. Implement what you learn and attempt to get results. Most of them won't work for you. That's not failure — it's data.
3. **Identify patterns, principles, and levers.** Across everything you try, what consistently moves the needle? These tend to be the things worth internalizing.
4. **Create your own process.** Tailor what you've learned to your specific lifestyle and situation. This is when agency becomes genuinely yours.
5. **Pass it down to others.** Teaching requires understanding — you cannot explain what you haven't truly internalized. The teacher learns more than the student.

The prerequisite for all of this: having something to pursue. Not certainty about what you want — most people don't know what they want. What works is a deep awareness of what you *don't* want, which creates an aim for the future. From there, you have a direction. Set a goal to make the aim more practical, and begin the cycle.

## Agency and Context Creation

Koe's framing of the AI threat is worth noting: AI is a tool. Tools need someone to wield them for a specific purpose. When AI generates content without a human vision behind it, there is no throughline, no theme, no personality, no context. The content is noise.

**Creators are context creators, not content creators.** The content is the vessel; context — your perspective, your vision, your trajectory — is what makes it mean anything. AI can generate the vessel; only you can supply the context. This is the same point as [[The Swap Test]] approached from the agency angle.
