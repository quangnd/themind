## Answered Questions

**How does this connect to deliberate practice (Ericsson)?**
Yes — it's the same mechanism. See [[Deliberate Practice]]. Ericsson's key finding: expertise comes from working just beyond current capacity, with feedback, repeatedly. Gioia's slow reading at 7 pages/day of Proust is deliberate practice: operating at the edge of comprehension capacity. His jazz piano learning was explicit about this. The [[Gioia's 12-Month Humanities Curriculum|12-month curriculum]] is also sequenced to maintain the challenge-skill balance — Week 5 (Aristotle's Poetics) makes Week 10 (Greek tragedy) legible.

**What is the minimal viable version?**
Strip the extreme; keep the structure. What survives: (1) **A protected daily reading block** — even 30 minutes, first thing, non-negotiable. (2) **Books chosen for difficulty**, not comfort — at least one genuinely hard book per year. (3) **Tracking what you read** — a simple list. (4) **Gap identification** — occasionally ask "what do I not know?" and make a short list. What's optional: orca-sized blocks, reading aloud, perfect sequential order. The core is: *protect time + choose difficulty + stay systematic*.

**Is "old books first, new books later" right for every field?**
No — it's domain-dependent. Strong case for tradition-first in: humanities, philosophy, literature, classical music (everything in conversation with prior work). Weaker case in: technology and software (foundational texts become outdated fast), natural sciences (current papers > historical ones for practice), business (context shifts too quickly). Hybrid approach for: psychology and self-knowledge (classical sources still highly relevant but so is current research). The underlying principle that generalizes: understand the *conversation* a field is having before opining on it.
