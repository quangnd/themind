# Evaluating Character

[[Ted Gioia]] paid a high price early in his life for trusting what people said rather than observing what they revealed through behavior. The 8 techniques he developed since are unified by a single principle: **the social performance is not the person**. To read someone accurately, you need to find the moments when the performance drops — when they're not trying to impress, not aware of being watched, or not in control of what they're revealing.

The deeper insight running through all eight techniques: character is expressed in *patterns of behavior*, not in stated values. And those patterns are most visible in unguarded moments — in small stakes situations, in how someone treats someone they don't need, in what irritates them, in how they improvise under pressure.

## Companion Framework

Gioia's [[Expertise vs. Credentials]] offers a parallel set of five tests specifically for evaluating *intellectual* credibility rather than character — predicting correctly, identifying issues early, saying truths insiders suppress, explaining what others find confusing, speaking bravely against powerful interests. Both frameworks share the same logic: pierce the social performance (credentials, titles, institutional rank) to find what's actually there.

## The 8 Techniques

### 1. Look at who they marry

A person's choice of closest lifelong partner is the single most revealing thing about them — more than anything they say, more than their public behavior, more than their professional record. It tells you what they think they deserve, what they secretly long for, and what they are willing to settle for. It is a choice made with the whole self, not the performed self.

> [!tip] In practice
> When you meet someone new at a professional event, spend at least a little time observing their partner. It will tell you things about them that no conversation could.

### 2. Watch how they treat service workers

When people interact with those who have no power and cannot return a favor, the social performance relaxes. They feel consequence-free, and the true self comes forward. How someone treats a waiter, a hotel employee, or anyone in a service role is one of the most reliable indicators of character you will ever witness.

Both directions matter: abuse of service workers is a reliable warning sign; extraordinary care and courtesy toward them is one of the strongest positive signals Gioia has encountered.

### 3. Ask about their early life

Character — and the capacity to handle challenge — is almost entirely formed in the first two decades of life. Gioia encountered a CEO who spent entire interviews asking about childhood and teenage years, never once discussing credentials or plans. His track record of accurately assessing people was impressive.

The implication: **the distance someone traveled before their formal credentials is more telling than the credentials themselves.** Growing up with adversity, learning to navigate the world without advantages — these shape character in ways that a smooth, well-supported trajectory does not.

### 4. Watch how they spend time and money

"I write articles and books, but the two most revealing documents about me are my calendar and monthly budget."

Words are easy; time and money are finite and precious. What a person actually allocates these to — not what they say they value, but where these resources actually go — reveals their real priorities at a deep level. This is true for everyone, without exception.

### 5. Notice what irritates them most in others

The flaw someone is most intolerant of in others is usually the flaw they struggle most with in themselves. When we see a weakness in someone else, they function as a mirror — and we are far more reactive to flaws we recognize (consciously or not) than to flaws we've never experienced.

Gioia calls this *uncanny* in its consistency. The cheater gripes about dishonesty. The person who can't listen complains that no one listens to them. The parent who made a terrible mistake absolutely loses it when they see their child about to repeat it.

> [!note] This works as self-knowledge too
> The things that make you angriest in others are often worth examining for what they reflect about yourself.

### 6. Watch whether they can listen

Great listeners are among the rarest people Gioia has encountered — and among the most effective. They can read a situation with accuracy, absorb what's actually happening rather than what they expect to be happening, and act in ways that maximize outcomes for everyone involved.

Most people either talk or do neither (checked out). The small subset who genuinely listen — who are fully present, who ask follow-up questions, who remember what you said — are invaluable and should be trusted with more than average.

Listening is systematically underrated because it looks passive. It is not. It is active comprehension under discipline.

### 7. Small dishonesty predicts large dishonesty

If someone will break a rule for something as trivial as moving a golf ball when no one is watching, they will break larger rules when higher stakes are involved. The scale changes; the character does not.

This is one Gioia emphasizes as a *warning sign to take seriously even when it seems petty*. The smallness of the transgression is precisely why it is revealing — there is no real reward, no real pressure. The person is showing you who they are in their most unconstrained state.

Don't dismiss this. The small test is the real test.

### 8. Watch how they handle unexpected problems

Improvisation reveals character. You cannot know how someone will behave under unscripted pressure until you see it. Some organizations stage mishaps during senior hiring interviews for this exact reason.

Two failure modes: over-reaction (explosion, loss of composure) and under-reaction (passivity, letting things spiral). The person who rises to the occasion — calm, decisive, neither controlling nor avoidant — is revealing something essential about their core values that no interview answer could.

## The Mirror Principle

Techniques 5 and 8 both involve a version of the same deeper idea: people reveal character most clearly in *uncontrolled moments* — when they're reacting rather than performing. Irritation is uncontrolled. Crisis response is uncontrolled. These are the moments to pay attention to.

## Applied to Yourself

Gioia closes by turning the framework inward. Every one of these techniques can be used for self-evaluation:

- Do you treat service workers fairly?
- What do you find yourself most irritated by in others?
- Can you actually listen, or are you waiting to speak?
- Are you trustworthy in small things?
- How do you handle unexpected problems?

> Perhaps the character you need to assess is your own. I take that idea seriously, although it's sometimes painful to use these evaluative methods on myself. But that, my friends, might be the most useful piece of advice of them all.

## Connections

These techniques are fundamentally about *epistemic practice* — how to know things about people that they won't tell you directly. In that sense they connect to the broader sensemaking concern in [[Epistemic Commons]]: how do we develop reliable methods for reading reality when so much of what we're offered is performance?

They also resonate with [[Note-Taking as Thinking]] — both are about the discipline of careful attention. Gioia's method of reading people is structurally similar to his method of reading books: look past the surface presentation for what's actually being revealed.
