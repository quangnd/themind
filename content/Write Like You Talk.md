# Write Like You Talk

[[Paul Graham]]'s simplest and most actionable essay on writing craft (2015). The core claim: if you write in spoken language, you'll be ahead of 95% of writers. The essay is itself a demonstration — it reads exactly like someone explaining an idea to a friend.

## The Problem

Something happens to most people when they start writing. They shift into a different register — more complex sentences, fancier words, more formal tone. Nobody says "the mercurial Spaniard" in conversation; people write whole books of it. This shift is not an upgrade. It's a loss.

Written language is worse than spoken language for three reasons:

1. **More complex = more work to read.** The reader has to parse longer, denser sentences. Attention drifts.
2. **More formal = more distance.** Formality gives the reader's attention *permission* to wander. Conversational tone holds it.
3. **False impression of depth.** Complex sentences and fancy words make the *writer* feel like they're saying more than they are. The complexity is a mask over thin ideas.

## The Counterintuitive Claim

> You don't need complex sentences to express complex ideas.

Graham observes that specialists discussing their own field don't use more complex sentences than when they talk about lunch. They use *different words*, but no more than necessary. And the harder the subject, the more informally experts speak — partly because they have less to prove, and partly because hard ideas can't afford to have language getting in the way.

> Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas.

## The Method

Two techniques, from light to drastic:

### The Friend Filter

Write your first draft however you normally would. Then go back through each sentence and ask: **"Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend?"** If not, imagine what you'd actually say, and use that instead.

Over time, this filter starts operating as you write. When you write something you wouldn't say, you hear the clank as it hits the page.

Graham's own practice: before publishing, he reads every essay out loud and fixes everything that doesn't sound like conversation. He even fixes phonetically awkward bits.

### The Nuclear Option

For writing so far removed from spoken language that sentence-by-sentence fixes won't save it: write the first draft, then **explain to a friend what you just wrote**. Replace the draft with what you said.

## Exceptions

Graham names three cases where spoken language is not the right register:

- **Poetry** — as much music as text; you can say things you wouldn't say in conversation
- **A handful of writers** who can get away with fancy prose (the exception, not the rule)
- **Deliberate obfuscation** — corporate bad-news announcements, or the bogus end of the humanities, where the point is to *not* be understood

For nearly everyone else, spoken language is better.

## Why This Matters

The essay's final observation is quietly devastating: people often tell Graham how much his essays sound like him talking. The fact that this seems *worthy of comment* shows how rarely anyone manages to write in spoken language. If everyone did it, the observation would be meaningless.

The implication: the bar is low. Most writers are competing in a field where almost nobody writes naturally. Simply removing the artificial formality puts you ahead of nearly all of them.

## Connections

**[[Conversational Authority]]** — Gioia's thesis about the cultural shift from scripted oratory to spontaneous dialogue is the *macro* version of Graham's *micro* writing advice. The same force is at work: audiences now trust conversational tone and distrust formality. Graham discovered this in his own writing; Gioia mapped it across all of culture. The connection is direct: Gioia noticed his Substack writing had become more conversational and couldn't explain it at first — he was enacting exactly what Graham prescribes.

**[[Clarity is Overrated]]** — an interesting tension. Gioia argues *against* the cult of clarity, claiming ambiguity and poetic thinking have value that analytical precision destroys. Graham argues *for* clarity via conversational simplicity. These are not contradictions — Graham's exceptions list (poetry, music-as-text) acknowledges the same territory Gioia defends. The difference: Graham is talking about prose that *should* communicate clearly but doesn't; Gioia is talking about prose that *shouldn't* be forced into clarity because the ideas are inherently ambiguous. The two essays have different enemies: Graham fights unnecessary complexity; Gioia fights sterile simplicity.

**[[Essays as Thinking Tools]]** — Graham is the canonical practitioner of essay-as-discovery. His conversational style is not incidental to the form — it's what makes the thinking visible. When writing sounds like talking, the reader can follow the thought as it develops. Formal academic prose hides the thinking behind the presentation.

**[[Ted Gioia's Reading Methodology]]** — Gioia reads aloud. Graham reads his essays aloud before publishing. The same technique, applied at different stages: Gioia reads to internalize; Graham reads to externalize. Both treat the ear as a quality filter that the eye misses.

> [!tip] The Friend Filter for non-native English writers
> For someone writing in a second language, the Friend Filter has a specific application: if you wouldn't say it in conversation with an English-speaking colleague, don't write it. This is especially relevant for business writing — reports, emails, status updates — where non-native speakers often over-formalize because they learned written English from textbooks, not from conversation. The chunks you use in meetings are closer to good writing than the formal phrases you learned in school.
