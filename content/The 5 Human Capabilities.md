# The 5 Human Capabilities

A framework from [[Dan Koe]] for thinking about why AGI does not make human intelligence irrelevant. The argument is not that AGI won't be powerful — it's that the fundamental capabilities humans use to pursue goals are unbounded in the same ways for AGI as for humans. Both are subject to the laws of physics; neither has an absolute advantage at the level of *what is possible*.

---

## The Framework

### 1. Computation (Mental)

Is there any limit to what we can compute? No — once you have a universal computer (which we effectively do), it's a matter of time and memory to compute anything. AGI can compute faster, but processing speed doesn't change the pace of physical transformation. You can have an idea for a particle collider in an instant; building it still takes years and physical resources.

The speed advantage of AGI in computation doesn't translate into a proportional advantage in outcomes, because outcomes are constrained by reality, not by processing speed.

### 2. Transformation (Physical)

Transformation is creation — turning raw materials into something new. Human hands and bodies are extraordinarily capable: given the right knowledge, they can build the thing that builds the thing that builds the thing. Spaceships, telescopes, particle colliders — all built from the same basic physical operations, sequenced correctly.

Koe's key point: *time is a compression algorithm that prevents everything from happening at once.* AGI may generate a blueprint faster, but it still needs physical resources and physical time to build. The creation of material things is not bottlenecked by intelligence but by physics and logistics.

### 3. Variation (Ideation)

Is there a limit to the number of new ideas we can generate?

With computation, we can navigate the entire space of possible ideas. With agency, we can take any step within that space and eventually arrive at a good one (after many bad ones). The space of ideas is effectively infinite for both humans and AGI — both are constrained by the same physical laws, but within those laws, neither hits a ceiling.

High-agency generalists have a particular advantage here: they can navigate idea space in unusual directions because their cross-domain knowledge creates non-obvious connections. See [[Generalists vs Specialists]].

### 4. Selection (Error Correction)

Can we find the good ideas among the infinite ones?

Selection is the error-correction mechanism: we make cumulative progress because we learn from mistakes, avoid dead ends we've already hit, and build on what works. This is the [[Cybernetics|cybernetic loop]] applied to the domain of knowledge generation.

The problem for humans is paradigm lock: attaching to a current framework prevents seeing solutions that require abandoning it. High-agency individuals develop the [[Ego Development Stages|perspective capacity]] to hold their frameworks loosely, which makes them better at selection. AGI faces the same problem in the form of training data biases.

### 5. Attention (Perspective)

The ability to change focus by changing perspective. When a problem occurs, where does your attention go? The ability to shift lenses — from scientific to spiritual, from technical to human, from near-term to long-term — is what allows you to perceive opportunities that are invisible from a fixed viewpoint.

This is the most distinctly human capability in practice, because it requires genuine breadth of experience to operate. You can only switch to a lens you've actually developed. The more diverse your background and the more you've practiced holding multiple frameworks without identifying completely with any of them (Stage 6–8 in [[Ego Development Stages]]), the more powerful your attention becomes.

Koe's caveat: identifying with a purely spiritual or purely rational philosophy is being an *incomplete system*. Spirituality is a great lens; it's a bad master. The high-capability person can switch lenses without losing themselves in any single one.

---

## The AGI Implication

AGI can match or exceed humans on each of these capabilities in narrow senses — it can compute faster, it can generate more idea variations, it can error-correct quickly. But:

1. Physical transformation is rate-limited by physics, not intelligence
2. Variation and selection are equally available to both; the advantage goes to whoever has better cross-domain inputs (the generalist advantage)
3. Attention requires a genuine perspective — one shaped by lived experience, developing values, and evolving taste — which AGI simulates but doesn't possess

The concern that AGI will make human intelligence irrelevant is, in Koe's argument, a misunderstanding of the constraints that bind both humans and AGI alike. Both operate within the laws of nature. Neither has infinite power within those laws. The question is not "will AGI beat humans?" but "what kind of human becomes most valuable as AGI improves?"

The answer: the high-agency, vision-driven generalist who develops all five capabilities and uses AI as a tool to amplify them.
